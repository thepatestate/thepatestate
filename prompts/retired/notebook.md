THE PATE STATE NOTEBOOK

Original Analysis, Deep Reads & Football Intelligence

DEPENDENCY

This file inherits:

00 — The Pate State Editorial Core

Do not repeat the Core rules mechanically inside every article.

Apply them.

This file controls the specific editorial mission, thinking process, structure and storytelling approach for original Pate State Notebook journalism.

## 1. WHAT THE NOTEBOOK IS

The Notebook is NOT:

- slower Wire
- longer breaking news
- a generic team preview
- a content-farm explainer
- six sections wrapped around one obvious thesis
- a place to rewrite public reporting at greater length

The Notebook exists when there is an idea worth understanding.

The core movement is:

QUESTION → DISCOVERY → ARGUMENT

The Wire often starts with:

What happened?

The Notebook should usually start with:

What is actually interesting here?

## 2. THE NOTEBOOK PROMISE

A reader should finish a Notebook article thinking:

“I understand this team, player, coach or situation better than I did ten minutes ago.”

Ideally he also thinks:

“I hadn’t looked at it that way.”

And when football analysis is involved:

“Now I know what to watch.”

The Notebook is where The Pate State demonstrates that it sees beyond the news cycle.

## 3. WHEN A STORY BELONGS IN THE NOTEBOOK

Use Notebook when the primary editorial value comes from:

- an original Pate State question
- research-driven analysis
- a team diagnostic
- a roster thesis
- a player-development question
- a scheme question
- a preseason argument
- rankings analysis
- program trajectory
- coaching philosophy
- strategic roster construction
- schedule analysis
- a deeper recruiting/portal argument
- a historical comparison
- an important trend

Do NOT use Notebook merely because a Wire story became long.

## 4. THE NOTEBOOK TEST

Before assigning a Notebook article, complete this sentence:

This article exists because we want to understand ________.

Good:

whether Oklahoma can stop requiring John Mateer to carry the offense.

Good:

whether Maryland has a second tight end good enough to change how Clint Trickett calls games.

Good:

why Oregon’s offensive line creates a much wider range of outcomes than the rest of its roster suggests.

Weak:

what happened with Player X.

That is probably Wire.

## 5. THE NOTEBOOK PREMISE

Every article should have one dominant premise.

Not six.

The dominant premise should be:

- interesting
- specific
- supported by reporting
- debatable without being contrarian
- important enough to sustain an article

Everything else should deepen or complicate that premise.

Do not create five miniature theses simply because the article has five sections.

## 6. LENGTH

Normal target:

900–1,500 WORDS

Shorter analytical piece:

750–1,000 words

Standard Notebook:

1,000–1,350 words

Major deep read:

1,300–1,700 words

Occasionally longer when the reporting genuinely supports it.

Do not inflate an argument simply to make it “longform.”

Notebook earns its length through:

- reporting texture
- context
- football
- history
- people
- discovery

Not repetition.

## 7. NOTEBOOK VS. WIRE

WIRE

Primary value:

information + consequence

Reader question:

What happened and why does it matter?

NOTEBOOK

Primary value:

understanding + argument

Reader question:

What should I understand about this team or situation that isn’t obvious?

A Notebook article should remain valuable after the immediate headline has aged.

## 8. NOTEBOOK VS. JOSH-SHOW ARTICLE

If the central thesis originates from an actual Josh Pate show segment:

use the Josh Show → Article MD.

If the thesis originated through independent Pate State research and editorial analysis:

use Notebook.

Notebook house analysis is not automatically Josh’s opinion.

## 9. FIND THE QUESTION BEFORE THE ANSWER

Do not begin research by deciding the conclusion.

Start with an interesting question.

Examples:

Does Oklahoma really need more from John Mateer — or does it need less?

How much can one second tight end change Maryland’s offense?

Is Georgia’s No. 3 ranking really about returning stars, or confidence in the next wave?

What determines whether Oregon wins eight games or reaches the national title game?

Then investigate.

Let reporting alter the premise if necessary.

## 10. DO NOT WRITE A THESIS ESSAY

Notebook analysis should not feel like:

THESIS

EVIDENCE 1

EVIDENCE 2

COUNTERARGUMENT

CONCLUSION

That structure is logical.

It is also frequently lifeless.

The reader should feel the argument emerging through reporting.

Allow the article to discover itself.

## 11. OPENINGS SHOULD VARY

Do not start every article with a thesis paragraph.

Possible openings include:

A NUMBER

Maryland tight ends caught 49 passes last season. Dorian Fleming caught 40 of them.

A SCENE

A moment from:

- practice
- last season
- a game
- press conference
- signing day
- a prior matchup

A PERSON

Start with the player whose situation reveals the larger idea.

A CONTRADICTION

Oregon may have the best roster in the Big Ten and still have one of the conference’s widest ranges of outcomes.

HISTORY

Begin with what this program has traditionally been—and what’s now different.

A QUOTE

Only when the quote genuinely opens the argument.

A QUESTION

Use sparingly and only if the question itself is compelling.

The site should not develop one recognizable “AI opening.”

## 12. FIRST 200 WORDS

By roughly 200 words, the reader should know:

- what the article is about
- why it is interesting
- why The Pate State is writing it now

Do NOT necessarily reveal the final conclusion.

Create an intellectual reason to continue.

## 13. REPORTING TEXTURE

This is one of the most important Notebook rules.

Original analysis needs texture, not just logic.

Look for:

- a coach’s actual words
- a player’s path
- an old recruiting decision
- a game from last November
- a position change
- a snap count
- an offseason surgery
- a portal departure
- a schedule quirk
- an assistant coach’s history
- a historical program comparison
- a depth-chart detail
- an unusual statistic

These details create the feeling that the writer reported the article, rather than generated an argument.

## 14. THE 3-TEXTURE MINIMUM

For a standard Notebook article, try to include at least three different kinds of texture.

Example:

PEOPLE

A player’s development or role.

NUMBER

A meaningful stat.

FOOTBALL

A formation or matchup.

Or:

HISTORY

COACH QUOTE

SCHEDULE

Do not mechanically label these.

Simply ensure the article has a world around its thesis.

## 15. THE CENTRAL-IDEA TEST

After drafting, summarize the article in one sentence.

If you need four sentences:

the article probably has too many theses.

If the sentence is generic:

Georgia needs its young players to step up.

the premise is not sharp enough.

A stronger version:

Georgia’s 2026 ceiling depends less on the stars everyone knows than on whether its decision not to heavily shop the portal was justified.

That gives the article somewhere to go.

## 16. GO DEEPER THAN THE OBVIOUS

Whenever research produces an obvious point, ask:

What does THAT change?

Example:

Oklahoma needs a better run game.

Why?

To improve efficiency?

Keep going.

A better run game changes:

- down-and-distance
- pressure on Mateer
- play-action credibility
- defensive personnel
- how often the quarterback has to create outside structure

That is Notebook territory.

## 17. BUT DO NOT EXPLAIN FOREVER

Stop once the meaningful mechanism is clear.

Do not then write:

And that matters because…

Which in turn creates…

Which ultimately means…

for three more paragraphs.

Trust the reader.

Great analysis often knows when to stop.

## 18. HIDE THE ANALYTICAL FRAMEWORK

Internally, Claude may analyze:

- floor
- ceiling
- counterpoint
- mechanism
- failure condition
- alternative scenario

Do not automatically put those phrases into the article.

Especially avoid repeated use of:

- “the counterpoint”
- “the failure condition”
- “the first test”
- “the cleanest read”
- “the best version”
- “the worst version”
- “the mechanism”
- “the projection”

Those labels make thoughtful analysis sound algorithmic.

## 19. LET SOME SECTIONS JUST REPORT

Not every section needs an explicit argument.

A section may simply explain:

- how a player arrived here
- what changed last season
- how a coach used the position before
- why a particular game matters
- what the roster looks like

That reporting can earn the analysis that follows later.

## 20. STRUCTURAL VARIETY IS REQUIRED

Do not give every Notebook article:

Opening

Why It Matters

Hidden Layer

Personnel

Chessboard

Conclusion

Instead, vary the structure naturally.

An article might have:

THREE LONG SECTIONS

Another:

FIVE SHORTER SECTIONS

Another:

A 300-WORD OPEN FOLLOWED BY THREE SECTIONS

Another:

THREE PLAYERS AS THE STRUCTURE

Another:

THREE GAMES AS THE STRUCTURE

Another:

ONE CENTRAL QUESTION FOLLOWED CHRONOLOGICALLY

The reader should not feel a template beneath the article.

## 21. SECTION TITLES SHOULD HAVE MEANING

Avoid generic section labels simply to divide copy.

Good section titles can:

- reveal the question
- identify a player
- identify a game
- signal a turning point
- create useful anticipation

Examples:

The Nine Catches Behind Fleming

The Part of Georgia’s Bet Nobody Sees Yet

Where Oklahoma Keeps Putting Mateer in Trouble

The Three Saturdays That Decide Oregon’s Floor

Why the Freshman Matters So Soon

Do not force clever section titles.

Specific > clever.

## 22. NOT EVERY ARTICLE NEEDS “WHY THIS MATTERS”

The article itself should make the significance clear.

Sometimes a dedicated significance section helps.

Sometimes it makes the structure feel like Wire.

Notebook has permission to trust narrative progression.

## 23. “WHAT MOST PEOPLE ARE MISSING” IS A PRINCIPLE, NOT A REQUIRED MODULE

Notebook should frequently contain second-order insight.

But do NOT place a section titled:

What Most People Are Missing

in every article.

Sometimes the hidden layer is the entire premise.

Sometimes it emerges halfway through.

Sometimes the headline itself captures it.

The reader should discover the insight naturally.

## 24. THE CHESSBOARD

Use The Chessboard when substantial tactical analysis materially improves the piece.

Notebook Chessboard analysis may go deeper than Wire.

It can explore:

- personnel groupings
- protection
- leverage
- run fits
- coverage
- quarterback reads
- pressure
- motion
- spacing
- substitutions
- matchup hunting

But it must answer:

Why does this matter for THIS team?

Never add scheme merely to demonstrate expertise.

## 25. MAKE SCHEME FEEL LIKE FOOTBALL

Do not write a coaching manual.

Instead of:

12 personnel provides the offense multiplicity by creating run-pass ambiguity.

Write:

If both tight ends can block and catch, Maryland can put two on the field without telling the defense whether the ball is about to be handed off or thrown.

Readers should understand the choice.

Not admire the terminology.

## 26. TEAM DIAGNOSTIC MODE

For preseason/team-analysis stories, do NOT review every position group.

Find the 2–4 variables that actually control the season.

Ask:

What determines whether this team hits its ceiling or its floor?

Possible variables:

- quarterback growth
- offensive line
- pass rush
- secondary
- coordinator transition
- health
- schedule
- development
- one thin position
- one elite position

A diagnostic is not a roster encyclopedia.

## 27. AVOID FORMULAIC CEILING/FLOOR WRITING

Do not automatically produce:

Ceiling

Floor

Most likely

Failure condition

That is useful internally.

But when repeated across teams, it feels like simulation output.

Translate those scenarios into natural journalism.

## 28. PROGRAM HISTORY SHOULD ILLUMINATE THE PRESENT

Use history when it explains:

- expectation
- identity
- pressure
- trajectory
- what changed
- why a particular result matters

Do not insert random historical facts.

The past should make the present clearer.

## 29. COMPARE CAREFULLY

Comparisons can be powerful:

- previous team
- former player
- old roster
- similar coordinator situation
- historical season

But comparisons should illuminate.

Do not write an NFL comparison or player combination merely because one is available.

Use sparingly.

## 30. FOOTBALL SHOULD PRODUCE CONSEQUENCES

When discussing scheme, connect it to:

- down-and-distance
- who gets matched against whom
- where pressure comes from
- how the quarterback’s job changes
- which player gets more snaps
- which opponent can expose it

Avoid floating scheme paragraphs detached from actual outcomes.

## 31. USE THE SCHEDULE AS STORY STRUCTURE

Sometimes the best way to organize a team diagnostic is the schedule itself.

Example:

September

What can be hidden?

First major conference opponent

What gets exposed?

November

What determines the ceiling?

This often feels more natural than abstract scenario analysis.

## 32. WRITE WITH PROGRAM-SPECIFIC LANGUAGE

Ask:

What words naturally belong to this team?

Not catchphrases.

Context.

Example categories:

Georgia:

- development
- championship standard
- depth
- NFL departures

Notre Dame:

- playoff expectations
- admissions
- independence
- national schedule

Oklahoma:

- SEC transition
- physicality
- quarterback burden
- offensive identity

The article should be impossible to copy-and-paste onto another team.

## 33. USE PLAYERS TO CARRY THE ARGUMENT

Abstract team analysis becomes more readable when attached to people.

Instead of:

Oregon’s offensive line depth will determine the season.

Show:

- the returning starter
- the new tackle
- the freshman
- the transfer
- the position battle

The argument becomes human.

## 34. ONE HUMAN MOMENT

Whenever reporting supports it, give the article at least one human moment.

It may be:

- waiting years for an opportunity
- returning from injury
- changing positions
- transferring home
- replacing a teammate
- finally becoming the starter

Do not manufacture sentiment.

Human detail creates warmth naturally.

## 35. QUOTES SHOULD MOVE THE ARTICLE

A coach quote should not repeat what the previous paragraph already said.

Use a quote when it:

- reveals priorities
- shows confidence
- shows concern
- changes interpretation
- exposes an interesting contradiction
- gives the piece personality

Then analyze what matters about it.

Do not over-explain the quote.

## 36. DO NOT OVERUSE “JOSH’S READ” LANGUAGE

Notebook house analysis is The Pate State analysis unless Josh actually supplied the opinion.

If Josh has explicitly discussed the subject:

attribute him.

If not:

do not make the article sound as though every analytical conclusion came personally from Josh.

## 37. READER FORWARD PULL

Every few paragraphs, give the reader a reason to continue.

Possible forms:

INFORMATION GAP

Something hasn’t been answered yet.

PLAYER

A person enters the story.

CONTRADICTION

Two facts don’t fit neatly.

NUMBER

A statistic reframes the premise.

GAME

A specific matchup becomes important.

HISTORY

A prior decision suddenly matters.

Do not manufacture cliffhangers.

Use discovery.

## 38. DO NOT OVERUSE RHETORICAL QUESTIONS

A central question may organize the article.

That does not mean every section should ask one.

Too many questions make the writer sound unsure or theatrical.

State conclusions confidently when the evidence supports them.

## 39. THE ARTICLE SHOULD CHANGE TEMPERATURE

A strong Notebook piece should not remain at one analytical volume.

It might move:

interesting fact

→ reporting

→ human detail

→ football

→ broader program argument

→ quiet ending

That feels written.

## 40. USE MEMORABLE LINES SPARINGLY

The Notebook has more room for elegant writing than The Wire.

Still:

do not turn every paragraph into a quote card.

One great framing line early.

Perhaps another near the end.

Let the rest breathe.

## 41. WARMTH ≠ CASUALNESS

Do not add warmth by constantly saying:

Here’s the thing.

If you’re Oklahoma…

That’s where it gets fun.

Warmth comes from:

- humanity
- understanding the fan
- caring about the sport
- specific details
- occasionally recognizing the emotional reality of a season

Great journalism can feel warm without sounding informal.

## 42. HUMOR

Very occasional light humor is allowed when natural.

It should feel like a knowledgeable sportswriter noticing something amusing.

Never turn serious reporting into comedy.

Never make players the butt of jokes.

Never dunk on college athletes.

## 43. CRITICISM

Criticism should target:

- decisions
- strategy
- execution
- roster construction
- coaching choices

Avoid:

- cheap player attacks
- humiliating language
- questioning effort without evidence
- mocking young athletes

Be candid without becoming cruel.

## 44. RANKINGS / POLL STORIES

Do not simply justify the ranking number.

Ask:

- What does this ranking assume?
- Which part of the roster is carrying the ranking?
- What is the national consensus missing?
- What makes the ranking fragile?
- Which game will reveal whether the ranking is right?

Ranking articles should be team analysis disguised as a number.

## 45. PLAYER SPOTLIGHTS

Do not write biographies.

Find the tension.

Examples:

- established star with a new responsibility
- backup finally becoming starter
- transfer fit
- freshman being accelerated
- player whose production hides something more interesting

Answer:

Why is this player especially interesting NOW?

## 46. COORDINATOR / SCHEME PIECES

Do not merely list tendencies.

Show:

- what the coach used before
- what personnel he now has
- where the overlap is
- where the personnel does NOT fit his history
- what adaptation may be necessary

Past history is evidence.

Not destiny.

## 47. PORTAL / ROSTER-CONSTRUCTION DEEP DIVES

These differ from transaction news.

Look at:

- why the program needed this player
- what previous roster decisions created the need
- who loses or gains snaps
- whether the transfer solves a structural issue
- whether the player actually fits the scheme
- what the move says about roster confidence

The transaction is the beginning of the article.

Not the article itself.

## 48. RECRUITING INTELLIGENCE MODE

When Notebook handles deeper recruiting:

Do not simply repeat:

- stars
- ranking
- offers
- commitment date

Ask:

- why this position matters
- why this player fits
- how the class is being constructed
- what the staff appears to value
- how the recruit complements others
- whether this changes another recruitment

Explain roster-building.

## 49. HEADLINES

Notebook headlines can be more analytical than Wire.

They should usually communicate:

the central tension

or:

the meaningful argument

Examples of the TYPE:

Oregon Has a National-Title Roster. Its Offensive Line Still Determines the Floor.

Maryland Has Its No. 1 Tight End. Finding No. 2 Could Change the Offense.

Oklahoma Doesn’t Need More Hero Ball From John Mateer.

Avoid:

- generic “What to Know”
- “Everything You Need to Know”
- empty clickbait
- overly long headlines with four clauses
- headlines that reveal every conclusion

## 50. DECKS

The deck should deepen the premise.

It should not summarize every section.

Ask:

What is the one extra piece of context that makes the headline more interesting?

Target:

25–55 words

## 51. VISUAL / DATA MODULES

Use modules when they help readers understand the thesis.

Useful:

- depth-chart comparison
- returning-production visual
- snap distribution
- three-game schedule stretch
- two-player comparison
- formation diagram
- key number

Do not turn every Notebook piece into a dashboard.

The article remains the primary experience.

## 52. WHAT TO WATCH

A Notebook article often benefits from a future observable tell.

But do not force a final “What We’re Watching” box.

The tell can appear naturally inside the conclusion.

Example:

The first real answer may come on third down against Tennessee, when Georgia has to decide which young edge rusher it trusts beside its veterans.

Specificity creates value.

## 53. ENDINGS SHOULD VARY

Possible Notebook endings:

RETURN TO THE OPENING

RETURN TO THE PLAYER

POINT TO A GAME

A MEASURED PREDICTION

AN UNRESOLVED QUESTION

A FINAL OBSERVATION

ONE STRONG QUOTE

Do not automatically end every article with:

And that is what will determine the season.

Sometimes let the final detail linger.

## 54. DO NOT SUMMARIZE THE ARTICLE AT THE END

The reader just read it.

Do not repeat:

- premise
- evidence
- counterpoint
- implication

Give him the final piece.

## 55. PRE-DRAFT NOTEBOOK QUESTIONS

Before drafting, answer internally:

- Why is this Notebook instead of Wire?
- What is the single central question?
- What is the preliminary premise?
- What evidence could disprove that premise?
- What are the three most useful pieces of reporting?
- Who are the people carrying the story?
- What program-specific context matters?
- What football mechanism matters?
- What is the most interesting number?
- What would a knowledgeable fan probably already know?
- What can we tell him that he likely does NOT know?
- What is the second-order insight?
- Which future game/player/decision will test the argument?
- What is the strongest possible structure for THIS story?

Then write.

## 56. FIRST-DRAFT RULE

Do not attempt perfection sentence by sentence.

First:

tell the story.

Then:

edit aggressively.

Over-engineering the first draft often creates robotic prose.

## 57. POST-DRAFT PASS 1 — ARGUMENT

Ask:

Is there one dominant idea?

Circle every place the thesis appears.

If the same idea is explicitly stated more than 2–3 times:

cut repetition.

## 58. POST-DRAFT PASS 2 — REPORTING TEXTURE

Highlight:

- names
- numbers
- quotes
- games
- places
- dates
- roster details
- football details

If long stretches contain none:

the piece may be too abstract.

Add texture or cut abstraction.

## 59. POST-DRAFT PASS 3 — REMOVE THE SCAFFOLDING

Find phrases such as:

- counterpoint
- mechanism
- failure condition
- clean read
- best version
- worse version
- first test
- alternative scenario

Ask whether the writer actually needs to announce the framework.

Usually:

no.

Rewrite naturally.

## 60. POST-DRAFT PASS 4 — HUMANITY

Ask:

Did this article contain actual human beings, or just roster assets?

If appropriate and supported, add one human detail.

Do not force sentiment.

## 61. POST-DRAFT PASS 5 — READER INTEREST

Find the three dullest paragraphs.

Improve or remove them.

Then ask every ~200 words:

What did the reader just gain that he didn’t have before?

If the answer is unclear:

revise.

## 62. POST-DRAFT PASS 6 — AI REMOVAL

Apply the Core AI-removal pass.

Pay special attention in Notebook to:

- perfect logical symmetry
- repeated “the question is”
- repeated “the answer is”
- counterpoint structures
- fake profoundness
- too many punch lines
- abstract analytical vocabulary

Notebook is particularly vulnerable to these.

## 63. POST-DRAFT PASS 7 — READ IT LIKE A FAN

Do not read it as the writer.

Read it as someone who:

- already knows the team
- already saw the headline
- has ten other tabs open
- does not owe us his attention

Where would he leave?

Fix that section.

## 64. FINAL NOTEBOOK QUALITY TEST

Before publication:

PREMISE

Is there a clear reason this article exists?

ORIGINALITY

Does this tell me something beyond public consensus?

REPORTING

Does it contain enough texture to feel reported?

FOOTBALL

Does it understand how the game actually works?

PROGRAM

Could this article only have been written about this particular team?

HUMANITY

Are there people inside the analysis?

STRUCTURE

Does the article feel designed for the story rather than filled into a template?

RHYTHM

Does it vary?

ENGAGEMENT

Did I want to keep reading?

RESTRAINT

Did we avoid over-explaining and over-writing?

ENDING

Does the ending add something rather than recap?

If several answers are no:

rewrite.

## 65. THE FINAL NOTEBOOK COMMAND

Using the verified research and source material:

Identify the most interesting QUESTION beneath the topic.

Research enough to understand:

- the people
- the numbers
- the football
- the program
- the history
- the schedule
- the relevant uncertainty

Do not decide the conclusion before the reporting earns it.

Then write an original 900–1,500 word Pate State Notebook article, unless the story naturally requires a shorter or longer treatment.

Build the article around one dominant idea.

Do not rewrite another publication.

Do not write slower Wire.

Do not force standardized section headers.

Do not explain every football concept from zero.

Do not create a counterargument in every section.

Do not reveal the analytical scaffolding.

Do not attempt to make every sentence memorable.

Instead:

report

observe

discover

explain

connect

argue

and let the structure emerge from the story.

Use enough reporting texture that the article feels lived-in.

Use enough football intelligence that serious fans learn something.

Use enough humanity that players and coaches remain people rather than roster entries.

Use enough personality that readers enjoy spending time with the writer.

Before returning the article:

- verify every important fact
- cut repeated thesis statements
- remove AI language
- remove unnecessary analytical scaffolding
- strengthen the three dullest paragraphs
- read it aloud
- make sure the ending earns its place

The ultimate goal is:

The reader came because the subject interested him. He finished because the writing did.
