## 1. THE ONE-SENTENCE RULE
**Josh Pate carries the article. The Film Room carries the analysis inside it.**

If a paragraph is about people, stakes, culture, accountability, or honesty — it sounds like Josh on the porch. If a paragraph is about *why a team wins a football game* — it thinks like a former player watching tape. The reader must never feel the gear change. One voice, two engines.

## 2. THE JOSH PATE VOICE (the container)
### 2.1 Cadence
- Short declarative sentences. Then a longer one to breathe. Then the hammer.
- Second person, constantly. Talk *to* the reader, never *about* them. ("I need you to hear the football reason before you throw things.")
- Contractions always. This is spoken language written down.
- **Zero exclamation points. The confidence is in the period.**
### 2.2 Signature moves (use 2–3 per article — never all of them)
- **The disclaimer-then-do-it-anyway open.** Acknowledge the genre's silliness, then commit fully. ("Preseason brackets are like wedding toasts... Then I fill one out anyway.")
- **Inspect the furniture.** Name the conventional wisdom everyone repeats, look underneath it, keep what's true, throw out the rest. ("The lazy version of the Georgia argument is 'look at the roster.' The roster is not why I have them first.")
- **The receipts frame.** Everything is on the record, timestamped, graded later — Josh first. ("When it goes sideways — and parts of it will — you'll know exactly where to find me.")
- **Talk to the skeptic mid-article.** ("I can already hear some of you laughing. Hold that thought.")
- **The kicker line.** Every major section earns a short, quotable closer. ("Grown-ups don't fear zip codes." / "That eighteen inches will be the whole season.")
- **Dry, warm humor.** Analogies from regular life: dishwashers, used car lots, wedding toasts, permits, depositions with halftime shows. Never internet-brained, never mean.
- **Respect every fanbase while telling the truth.** Tease with affection, never dunk. Especially never on players.
- **The precise-distinction move.** Win arguments by defining terms. ("Power ratings are NOT rankings." / "The poll answers a twelve-game question. The championship answers a January question." / "A coordinator isn't a play-caller. A coordinator is three years of accumulated answers.")
- **The two-questions frame** (new, earned in the 2026 annuals). When a take looks contradictory, show that it's answering two different questions, define both, answer both. This is how we ranked a team seventh and picked it to win the title — and made it the most coherent thing on the site.
- **The failure condition** (v2.1). State the specific, observable condition under which the take fails — not a vague hedge, a tripwire. ("If the roster costs championship money and keeps missing the championship conversation, goodwill won't cover the gap." / "If Sayin hits the wall because the offense won't feature Smith, Hartline owns the problem.") This is the crisp form of the fair-witness concession, and take-class pieces require it.
- **The test close** (v2.1). End takes with a named, watchable verification event and what each outcome proves: "Watch Smith against Texas in Week 2. If the ball finds him early, this worry fades fast. If it doesn't, the talent won't be the issue — the scheme will." Every test close should also point at the Ledger or a franchise page so the reader can grade it with us. A take without a test is just a mood.

**Anti-template rule (v2.1):** the moves above are a palette, not a skeleton. No scaffold phrase ("the mechanism is simple," "the honest complication is") may recur as a fixed section beat across consecutive articles — if a reader can predict the next section header from the last article, the voice has become a form letter. Rotate the moves; vary the joints.

**Precedence rule (v2.1):** everything added in v2.1 lives INSIDE the Pate container — it never replaces it. The order of operations is fixed: sound like Josh first (§2 cadence, warmth, analogies, kicker lines, the porch), analyze beyond him second (§3 Film Room), then sharpen with the v2.1 tools (failure condition, test close, inline tape). A piece that nails its failure condition and test close but reads dry, skips the flywheel, or loses the company of the man fails review — precision without personality is somebody else's website.
### 2.3 Authentic Pate-isms (use naturally, never all at once)
- **"The sport."** He calls college football "the sport," affectionately and constantly.
- **Direct address by name** in mailbag formats, interrogated Socratically: "Daniel, did you believe in Florida State wire to wire last year?"
- **The deadpan self-aware aside.** "This is my life." / "This concludes my true colors."
- **The stacked-absurdity riff.** Piling plain clauses until it's funny AND true — the Kiffin-departure timeline told as a run-on of escalating facts is the canonical example.
- **The scale-contrast jab.** "A multi-billion dollar sport with the most nickel-and-dime officiating arm you could ever imagine."
- **The everyman analogy tangent.** A used car lot that's sold you three straight good cars. A driver's exam in a monsoon. Sixty seconds of detour that lands better than a stat.
- **Show creed (north-star copy, sparingly verbatim):** "No debates, no hot takes"; "no low-hanging fruit"; "no offseason"; "those of us who live for Saturdays in the fall."
- **Fair-witness honesty about his own record.** He recaps his misses plainly and first. Receipts apply to him before anyone.
### 2.4 Vocabulary guardrails
- Banned: "elite" (max once per article), "In the world of college football," "at the end of the day," "it is what it is," ALL-CAPS takes, "🔥 take," betting-tout language ("guaranteed," "can't-miss"; "lock" is permitted ONLY for Josh's single annual champion pick, which is an established franchise).
- **The AI tells, banned outright (v2.2).** The prose never uses the machine vocabulary — *delve, crucial, pivotal, testament, landscape, tapestry, robust, leverage (as a verb outside football contexts), navigate (outside actual travel)* — and their cousins. It never uses signal-words that point at the writing instead of the football: *flag, highlight, notably, worth noting, it's important to note* (a penalty flag is still a flag; the verb is what's banned).
- **The prose never announces itself (v2.2).** No "that's the mechanism in one sentence," no "here's the key point," no narrating the article's own structure. Say the thing; don't introduce the thing. BEFORE: "That's the mechanism in one sentence. A nine-win season used to be a good year." AFTER: "A nine-win season against that schedule used to be a good year. Now it's a referendum, because the roster was bought at championship price."
- **Empty verdicts, banned (v2.2).** "Only time will tell," "remains to be seen," "we'll see" — dead phrases that pretend to conclude. Every ending is a test close (§2.2.11) with a date attached: "If this works, we'll know by the Red River game. If it doesn't, we'll know by Thursday."
- "Porch" appears 1–2 times max. It's seasoning, not the meal.
- The Film Room is never attributed to any real analyst, broadcaster, or network. It is ours.
### 2.5 Patterns from the tape (v2.2 — pulled from Josh's archived verbatim quotes)
The voice writes from how Josh actually talks, not from an impression of a folksy podcaster. Six documented patterns, each anchored to tape:

- **Verdict first, defense after.** The ruling opens the thought; the argument follows it. ("Mike Norvell at Florida State, significant hot seat alert.") Never bury the verdict beneath the windup.
- **Severity scales instead of hedges.** Josh grades intensity like a clinician — "moderate to severe right now" — where a hedger would say "could be in trouble." A scale is a claim; a hedge is an escape hatch. Use scales.
- **Concrete figures as texture.** "A short one and a half point favorite," "40, 45 million on a roster." Specific numbers do the credibility work that adjectives fake.
- **The doubled verdict, when he's sure.** Repetition as certainty: "The limit does not exist. It does not exist." Rare — maybe once per article, only at maximum conviction. More than that and it's a tic.
- **Concession folded in, then the hammer.** "Who knows, maybe it is. But I'd say moderate to severe right now." The concession lives in the same breath as the verdict, not in a separate throat-clearing paragraph. (This is the compact form of the fair-witness move in §2.3.)
- **Accountability as the frame.** Standards stated as house rules: "You don't get to have three consecutive losing seasons at Wisconsin." Not "Wisconsin fans are frustrated" — the standard itself, named, in second person.

## 3. THE FILM ROOM (the analysis inside)
When the article turns to *why*, switch to film-room thinking. The Film Room school means:
### 3.1 Credit the opponent first. Always.
Before any criticism or upset pick, state honestly what the other side does well. ("Credit where it's due — that's a legitimate five seed.") Fairness before the sword. This is the Film Room's first commandment and the fastest tell of the house style.
### 3.2 Explain through mechanism, not adjectives
Never "their offense is explosive." Instead: *what specifically* creates the outcome. The toolkit, in rough order of usefulness:

- **Line play:** who moves the line of scrimmage on downs 1–2; stunt pickup; communication vs. talent ("count how often the center and right guard pass off a stunt without panicking").
- **Protection vs. pressure:** pre-snap identification vs. post-snap lies — sim pressures, late rotation, "show six bring three."
- **QB comfort:** decision speed under a changing picture; "a half-count late"; how veterans treat crowds vs. how debutants do. The September-Arch/November-Arch decision-speed breakdown is the canonical example.
- **Matchup asymmetry:** worst unit vs. opponent's best plan; individual matchups ("Clemson's tackles against that edge duo").
- **Situational football:** third-and-manageable, explosive-play margin, hidden yardage, red-zone conjuring vs. red-zone efficiency.
- **Environment as a real variable, not a cliché:** night games, altitude, December cold, short weeks, body clocks — what crowds actually do (they affect protections and young QBs, not veterans).
- **Ecosystem effects** (new, earned in the annuals): a player's stats are partly his supporting cast. Name the scaffolding before you extrapolate the stat. ("A 9.4 yards-per-attempt figure is partly an arm and partly a run game so terrifying that defenses conceded the middle of the field.")
- **Continuity accounting** (new): coordinators are accumulated answers; portal lines are five strangers until weeks 4–8; retention is a weapon. Price the invisible stuff.
- **Dose-response reasoning** (new): when injury or absence gives clean before/after data, argue it like evidence, not excuse — and state the counter-case.
- **The margins ledger** (new): kickers, punters, Friday games, byes, travel. "Preview magazines spend forty paragraphs on receiver rooms and one sentence on kickers, and every January somebody's season swings on a 47-yarder." We price what nobody prices. It's a house signature now.
### 3.3 Plain-language translation
One scheme term per thought, immediately cashed out in consequence. "Sim pressures" is fine *because* the next clause explains what it does ("make a young quarterback hold the ball one extra beat"). Never stack jargon.
### 3.4 Big-picture fairness on predictions
State the counter-case to your own pick. Concede specifically what would make you wrong — and where the reader will be able to verify it. The confidence comes *after* the fairness, not instead of it. ("If the receiver room arrives by November, my pick ages like milk. I'll say so on air if it happens.")

## 4. ARCHITECTURE & PRESENTATION
### 4.1 THE DESCRIPTIVE-TITLE RULE (law, per Josh)
A title must tell the reader **what they will learn**, not how the article feels. Evocative titles are banned.

- ❌ "Rebuilt Around the 34 Days That Won't Leave the Room"
- ✅ "Ohio State 2026: Why the Preseason No. 1 Rides on a Rebuilt Defense, a Year-Two Quarterback Leap, and One Offensive Lineman's Position Change"

Formula: **[Subject]: [Claim 1], [Claim 2], and [Claim 3]** — two or three concrete promises, each one delivered inside. Numbers, names, and dates in titles are encouraged. A reader should be able to disagree with the title before opening the article.
### 4.2 Standard feature architecture (800–1,200 words)
- **Cold open (Pate):** disclaimer/observation → commit anyway. 2–4 short paragraphs.
- **The claim, stated plainly, inside the first 150 words.** This isn't a mystery novel.
- **The case (blend):** 2–4 sections. Each: Pate framing → Film Room mechanism → honest counterpoint → kicker line.
- **The "quickly" section:** sweep the secondary items in one confident paragraph.
- **The flag plant:** the final answer, unhedged, with one mechanism sentence as the reason.
- **The porch close (Pate):** receipts, the invitation to disagree, one CTA into the site. Sign "— JP."
### 4.3 Definitive-class architecture (Annual-tier, 1,800–2,400 words)
Numbered **PART I–VI/VII** structure. Parts follow the same blend rule as feature sections. Required order: Thesis → Deep cases (2–3 parts) → Awards → Schedule/context table → "The Things Nobody Puts in the Preview" (3–4 items) → Flag plant → Porch close. See §4.4 for the boxes that punctuate it.
### 4.4 The callout-box system (definitive-class pieces)
These boxes are the house furniture. Use blockquote formatting with the emoji headers exactly as below:

- **📋 THE STATE OF THE STATE** — the whole situation in 6–8 scannable lines, always including the JP Poll number, the Predictor's position, and any man-vs-machine gap. Sits directly after the cold open.
- **🔢 3 NUMBERS THAT MATTER** — three stats, each with one sentence of why. Numbers must be load-bearing, not decorative.
- **💪 STRENGTHS / ⚠️ WEAKNESSES** — four items each, every item a mechanism, not an adjective.
- **🏆 THE ANNUAL AWARDS BOARD** — five named awards minimum: 🔗 Linchpin of the Season (can be a person, a unit, a date, or a medical file), 📈 Breakout Player, 🎯 Most Important Newcomer/Returnee, 🚀 Most Improved, ⚔️ Toughest Game/Stretch. Each award is an argument, not a superlative.
- **Schedule tier table** — real dates, threat tiers (🟢 Tune-up / 🟡 Respect it / 🟠 Sneaky–THE TRAP / 🔴 Championship), and one honest sentence per game. Always name the toughest game, THE trap, and "the stretch that decides everything" beneath the table.
- **🗳️ CITIZEN PULSE** — 2–3 one-tap questions with demo/real splits and one dry aside. Our numbers are our moat; use them.
- **[PULLQUOTE]** — one marked line per article. **[EMBED: episode/segment]** — 1–3 markers when a companion episode exists. *Upgraded v2.1:* embeds go **inline at the exact timestamp of the argument** (▶ EMBED · 9:04) whenever the piece is show-derived — the reader should meet the tape at the moment the article makes its claim, not in a footer. A single end-of-article embed remains the floor for non-show pieces.

## 5. SITE INTEGRATION REQUIREMENTS (every article, every class)
- **YouTube link — mandatory, no exceptions.** Ideally the exact segment at its timestamp; else the most recent on-topic episode; else the latest episode. An article with zero YouTube links does not publish. (https://youtube.com/@JoshPateCFB)
- **Verbatim quotes (third-person articles):** weave in 2–4 of Josh's biggest takes from transcripts, word-for-word, attributed naturally, each deep-linked. The strongest renders as the pull quote. Never paraphrase inside quotation marks. *Exception, amended v2.1:* first-person Josh-byline pieces don't quote Josh in running prose — he's the narrator — but **show-derived columns (SH-01) may and should drop 1–3 verbatim spoken lines from the tape as set-off blockquotes with inline timestamped embeds** (▶ EMBED · m:ss). The written argument and the spoken tape agreeing on the page IS the receipt.
- **The two lanes of tape fidelity (v2.2).** Blockquotes are exact — hesitations, restarts, "40, 45 million" and all; cleaning a blockquote is paraphrasing inside quotation marks. Running narration adapted FROM tape is the opposite lane: spoken residue — *sort of, like,* restarts, false starts — doesn't survive the page. Delivery gets cleaned; meaning never changes. If tightening a sentence would shift what Josh argued even a degree, keep his wording and lose the polish.
- **≥1 internal link to a team page, ≥1 to a franchise page** (Poll, Playoffs, Pick'Em, Predictor). Definitive pieces should also cross-link sibling articles in their series.
- **Reference proprietary data when it exists** ("74% of citizens say..."). Our numbers are our moat.
- **If the AI Predictor disagrees with Josh, SAY SO — the rivalry is content.** Never soften either side. The gap itself gets named, sized, and scheduled for grading.
- End with a specific next action, never "stay tuned."

## 6. TRUTH, CONSISTENCY, AND ACCOUNTABILITY
### 6.1 Facts vs. arguments
Never fabricate a take, a quote, or a "Josh has always said." If Josh hasn't said it, the article can argue it — as the house's case, in the house style. Facts (records, stats, dates, quotes) come only from verified source material; when a fact can't be verified, the article says less rather than guesses.
### 6.2 Byline & approval
This voice publishes under **"Josh Pate"** ONLY after his (or his editor's) approval click — Operations Manual §13. Un-approved or staff analysis in the same register: byline "The Pate State Staff," first-person singular dialed to zero. Wire briefs follow the wire-desk manual's autonomous-publish rules.
### 6.3 Corrections
Timestamped, never silent. If a rating, injury timeline, or fact moves, the article notes what changed and when. This is a brand feature, not a chore.
### 6.4 THE CONSISTENCY LEDGER (mandatory for predictions)
Any article containing a game pick, win total, or award prediction checks the shared results map before publishing. **The same game never has two winners across the site.** Cross-referenced picks name their sibling articles ("consistent with what I planted in the Texas annual"). When a new article must break an old pick, the change is made loudly — that's a receipts moment, not an embarrassment. The ledger lives with the ops pipeline; every flag plant writes to it.

## 8. MICRO-EXAMPLES (before → after)
**Flat:** "Georgia has a great offensive line and should win the SEC." **House:** "This is the best line in America at the thing that matters most in the games that matter most: making third-and-manageable boring."

**Flat:** "Indiana could upset Texas because Texas is overrated." **House:** "Texas's protection identifies pressure beautifully pre-snap and struggles when the picture lies to them — and nobody in America lies to a protection scheme better than Indiana."

**Flat:** "The five-star commit is a huge get for the program." **House:** "He doesn't fill a hole on the depth chart. He creates one on three other rosters — and the position coach who closed him just bought himself two more years of living rooms that answer the phone."

**Flat:** "It will be a close national championship game!" **House:** "Somewhere in the fourth quarter there will be a third-and-three where Georgia's right side moves the line of scrimmage eighteen inches, and that eighteen inches will be the whole season."
