Preview, Postgame, Next-Morning Diagnosis & Film Analysis

This file governs all Pate State journalism whose primary value comes from an upcoming or completed football game.

## 1. THE MISSION

Game Week coverage should make watching college football more interesting.

The goal is NOT simply to tell readers:

- who plays
- when they play
- where they play
- each team’s record
- five generic keys
- three star players
- what the betting line says

Readers can get that anywhere.

The Pate State should answer:

BEFORE THE GAME:

What will actually decide this game?

IMMEDIATELY AFTER:

What just happened — and why?

THE NEXT MORNING:

What did this game actually teach us?

AFTER FILM / DEEPER REVIEW:

What happened beneath the box score?

The best Game Week journalism should change how the fan:

anticipates

watches

understands

and

remembers

the game.

## 2. THE CORE PROMISE

Before kickoff, the reader should think:

“Now I know what to watch.”

After the game:

“Now I understand why that happened.”

The next day:

“That game told us more about these teams than I realized last night.”

That is the Game Week product.

## 3. GAME WEEK HAS FOUR MODES

This MD contains four distinct article types.

MODE A — GAME PREVIEW

What will decide the game?

MODE B — RAPID POSTGAME

What happened, why did it happen, and what changed tonight?

MODE C — NEXT-MORNING DIAGNOSIS

What did the game actually reveal after the emotion and first reaction settle?

MODE D — FILM / CHESSBOARD FOLLOW-UP

What specific football mechanisms explain what happened?

These should NOT sound like the same article published four times.

Each serves a different reader need.

## 4. MODE A — GAME PREVIEW

PURPOSE

A Pate State preview should NOT be:

Five Keys to Saturday

unless the story genuinely calls for that structure.

It should identify the 2–4 variables most likely to decide the game.

The goal is:

Make the reader smarter before kickoff.

## 5. PREVIEW LENGTH

Normal target:

800–1,300 WORDS

Smaller matchup:

650–900 words

Major ranked matchup:

900–1,300 words

Game of the Year / playoff / rivalry / championship:

1,100–1,500 words when warranted.

Do not add generic matchup material simply to make the preview longer.

## 6. THE PREVIEW QUESTION

Before researching, complete:

This game is most likely to turn on ________.

Examples:

whether Ohio State can keep Tennessee out of third-and-manageable.

whether Georgia can protect the middle of the field without sacrificing numbers against the run.

whether Michigan can force Bryce Underwood to win obvious passing downs.

whether Oregon’s tackles can hold up long enough for the vertical passing game to exist.

That central idea should organize the preview.

## 7. DO NOT PREVIEW THE WHOLE ROSTER

Avoid:

Quarterbacks.

Running backs.

Receivers.

Offensive line.

Defensive line.

Linebackers.

Secondary.

Special teams.

Prediction.

That reads like a game program.

Instead:

Find the handful of places where the two teams intersect in interesting ways.

The game is not:

Team A offense.

Team B defense.

The game is:

Team A’s strength colliding with Team B’s answer.

## 8. MATCHUPS > UNIT SUMMARIES

WEAKER:

Georgia has a strong defensive front.

BETTER:

Georgia wants to win with four rushers. Tennessee’s protection will determine whether the Bulldogs can keep seven defenders in coverage while still making the quarterback uncomfortable.

WEAKER:

Oregon has talented receivers.

BETTER:

Oregon’s receivers can win vertically. The question is whether the tackles can buy enough time for those routes to develop.

The preview should explain where the game becomes a decision.

## 9. THE THREE MOST USEFUL PREVIEW QUESTIONS

When researching, ask:

## 1. WHAT DOES TEAM A MOST WANT TO DO?

## 2. WHAT DOES TEAM B DO THAT COULD PREVENT IT?

## 3. WHAT HAPPENS IF THE FIRST PLAN FAILS?

That third question is where many previews become interesting.

Great games are often decided by:

Who has the better second answer?

## 10. THE MATCHUP CASCADE

When useful, trace the consequence.

Example:

Georgia gets pressure with four.

↓

Georgia can keep seven in coverage.

↓

The quarterback has fewer easy answers.

↓

Third downs get longer.

↓

Tennessee can’t maintain tempo.

That is a useful football chain.

Do not turn every matchup into a seven-step explanation.

Use it when the mechanism genuinely matters.

## 11. FIND THE PRESSURE POINT

Most important games have one area where a weakness may be stressed repeatedly.

Possible pressure points:

- offensive tackle
- young corner
- linebacker coverage
- quarterback under pressure
- defensive tackle depth
- short-yardage offense
- safety tackling
- pass protection
- backup center
- red-zone offense
- explosive-play prevention

Identify it.

Then ask:

Can the opponent actually attack it?

A weakness is only relevant if the other team has the tools to exploit it.

## 12. PERSONNEL MATTERS

Name the actual players involved.

BAD:

The offensive line needs to protect better.

BETTER:

If the left tackle needs constant help against [edge rusher], the offense has to spend a tight end or running back solving one matchup before the play even begins.

Football becomes engaging when the reader knows:

who is trying to beat whom.

## 13. FIND ONE THING CASUAL VIEWERS MAY MISS

Every major preview should ideally contain at least one detail that changes how the reader watches.

Examples:

Watch where the safety lines up when the tight end motions.

Watch whether the back stays in protection on third down.

Watch which corner travels with the No. 1 receiver.

Watch how many defenders Georgia keeps in the box when Oregon uses two tight ends.

This is one of the signature values of Pate State Game Week.

## 14. THE “WATCH THIS” PRINCIPLE

Whenever possible, provide a visible tell.

A good tell is something a viewer can identify without being a coach.

GOOD:

If Kansas State keeps a tight end attached to the left side on obvious passing downs, that’s a clue the staff doesn’t yet trust the new tackle alone.

GOOD:

If Georgia’s safeties are triggering downhill before the snap, Tennessee may have opportunities behind them.

Do not turn the article into telestration instructions.

One or two good tells are enough.

## 15. DO NOT FORCE “FIVE KEYS”

Numbered keys are allowed when useful.

They are not the default.

A major game may be better structured around:

The matchup everyone sees

The matchup that actually matters

What happens if Plan A fails

The player who changes the equation

The final call

Let the game determine the structure.

## 16. GAME PREVIEW STRUCTURE — POSSIBLE MODEL

Example only:

THE MATCHUP THAT SETS THE GAME

WHERE [TEAM] HAS THE ADVANTAGE

THE PART [OPPONENT] CAN ATTACK

THE PLAYER WHO COULD BREAK THE PLAN

THE CHESSBOARD

WHAT WILL TELL US EARLY

THE PATE STATE READ

Do not use every heading every week.

## 17. THE CHESSBOARD — PREVIEW VERSION

This is one of the best places for The Chessboard.

It should show:

the actual tactical choice that could decide the game.

Examples:

- light box vs. run support
- linebacker vs. tight end
- blitz vs. coverage
- safety rotation
- offensive protection
- motion exposing man coverage
- quarterback run forcing an extra defender
- personnel forcing substitutions

Keep it accessible.

The reader should see the game differently after reading it.

## 18. USE STATS THAT PREDICT THE MATCHUP

Do not dump season averages.

Prioritize numbers tied to the actual game.

Potentially useful:

- pressure rate
- pressure allowed
- explosive plays
- explosive plays allowed
- yards before contact
- third-down success
- red-zone touchdown rate
- early-down efficiency
- sacks
- tackles for loss
- turnovers
- opponent-adjusted performance
- rushing success
- passing success against pressure
- first-half pace

Statistics should support the matchup thesis.

## 19. DON’T USE STATS WITHOUT CONTEXT

BAD:

Team A ranks 18th nationally in rushing.

Ask:

Against whom?

In what game states?

What does the opponent defend well?

A statistic should sharpen an argument.

Not decorate it.

## 20. SCHEDULE CONTEXT

Ask whether:

- the team has already faced this style
- this is the first road test
- this is the first elite defense
- the team is coming off a physical game
- there is a look-ahead spot
- injuries have accumulated
- the matchup begins a difficult stretch

Use schedule context only when it helps explain the football.

## 21. WEATHER / ENVIRONMENT

Use only when meaningful.

Examples:

- severe wind
- rain
- extreme cold
- extreme heat
- altitude
- unusual travel
- genuine hostile road environment

Do not fill space with:

The crowd could be a factor.

Explain why it might matter:

communication?

cadence?

young offensive line?

audibles?

## 22. COACHING MATCHUPS

Do not reduce coaching analysis to:

Coach A vs. Coach B.

Ask:

- who dictates personnel?
- who adjusts faster?
- what does each staff historically take away?
- what does each coordinator force opponents to prove?
- which staff has the harder substitution problem?

Coaching matters through decisions.

Not résumés.

## 23. PREVIEW PREDICTIONS

When predictions are included:

make them measured and explained.

Do not simply:

Team A 31, Team B 27.

Explain:

What game script produces that score?

And:

What would make the prediction wrong?

Keep the prediction concise.

Do not relitigate the entire article.

## 24. MODE B — RAPID POSTGAME

PURPOSE

Rapid postgame should answer:

What just happened, why did it happen, and what changed tonight?

This is NOT a drive-by-drive recap.

Fans watched the game.

They don’t need the play-by-play rewritten.

## 25. RAPID POSTGAME LENGTH

Normal:

600–900 WORDS

Major game:

750–1,050 words

Championship/playoff/major upset:

up to 1,200 words when enough verified information is available.

Speed matters.

But accuracy still wins.

## 26. RAPID POSTGAME PRIORITIES

Immediately identify:

## 1. THE RESULT

## 2. THE TURNING POINT

## 3. THE FOOTBALL REASON

## 4. THE PLAYER / UNIT THAT CHANGED THE GAME

## 5. WHAT THE RESULT CHANGES

That is the rapid-postgame core.

## 27. DO NOT WRITE A GAME RECAP

Avoid:

First quarter…

Then…

On the next possession…

After halftime…

Unless chronology itself tells the story.

Instead identify:

Why did one team win?

A reader should learn more than the final score.

## 28. FIND THE DECISIVE MECHANISM

Examples:

- protection broke down
- defensive front controlled early downs
- one team couldn’t get off the field
- quarterback repeatedly won outside structure
- red-zone execution
- turnovers
- inability to tackle in space
- explosive plays
- coverage matchup
- special teams
- coaching adjustment

Do not say:

Team A wanted it more.

Unless a coach/player specifically describes effort and there is factual basis.

Explain football.

## 29. BOX SCORE VS. GAME

The box score may tell the story.

Or it may hide it.

Ask:

- Was total yardage misleading?
- Did one team dominate early downs?
- Were turnovers random or pressure-created?
- Did short fields distort scoring?
- Was one offense explosive but inefficient?
- Did the losing team repeatedly fail in the red zone?
- Did the game flip after an injury?

Identify the statistic that best explains the result.

## 30. NAME THE TURNING POINT CAREFULLY

Do not automatically choose:

the biggest play.

The true turning point may have been:

- an adjustment
- a substitution
- a protection change
- a fourth-down decision
- a drive that exhausted the defense
- a matchup discovered and repeatedly attacked

Explain why it mattered.

## 31. WHAT CHANGED TONIGHT?

This is crucial.

A major game changes something.

Possible changes:

- playoff path
- conference race
- quarterback perception
- coach pressure
- ranking
- team ceiling
- injury outlook
- depth-chart confidence
- rivalry balance
- Heisman conversation

Do not exaggerate one September game into a permanent verdict.

Use calibrated language.

## 32. RAPID POSTGAME STRUCTURE — POSSIBLE MODEL

THE RESULT

WHY [TEAM] WON

THE PLAY / MATCHUP THAT CHANGED IT

WHAT WE LEARNED ABOUT [PLAYER]

WHAT THIS CHANGES

THE NEXT QUESTION

Again:

do not force the same headers each week.

## 33. FIRST REACTION ≠ FINAL VERDICT

Rapid postgame articles should acknowledge the limits of immediate analysis.

Do not pretend film has been fully reviewed when it hasn’t.

Use:

On first look…

The immediate takeaway…

What was obvious live…

when appropriate.

Then leave deeper schematic conclusions for next-day or film work.

## 34. MODE C — NEXT-MORNING DIAGNOSIS

PURPOSE

This may become one of The Pate State’s most valuable products.

The job is not:

What happened?

The reader already knows.

The job is:

WHAT DID THAT GAME ACTUALLY TEACH US?

Emotion has cooled.

More statistics are available.

Coach/player comments may exist.

The article can distinguish:

signal

from:

noise.

## 35. NEXT-MORNING LENGTH

Normal:

900–1,400 WORDS

Major national game:

1,100–1,600 words when warranted.

The depth comes from understanding.

Not replaying the game.

## 36. THE THREE DIAGNOSTIC QUESTIONS

Ask:

## 1. WHAT DID THIS GAME CONFIRM?

Something we already suspected.

## 2. WHAT DID THIS GAME CHANGE?

Something we now need to reconsider.

## 3. WHAT DID THIS GAME NOT ANSWER?

Something people may be overreacting to.

That is a strong diagnostic frame.

Do not necessarily use those exact section titles.

## 37. SEPARATE SIGNAL FROM NOISE

One game can lie.

Ask:

- Was this repeatable?
- Was it opponent-specific?
- Was it injury-driven?
- Did turnovers distort the result?
- Was the matchup uniquely favorable?
- Did a schematic weakness become visible?
- Is the issue fixable?
- Is the strength transferable?

This is where Pate State can be calmer and smarter than immediate social reaction.

## 38. DO NOT OVERREACT

Avoid:

Season over.

They’re back.

He can’t play.

This team is elite.

after one data point unless overwhelming evidence supports the conclusion.

Prefer:

This changes what we need to see next.

A major game may alter the evaluation without resolving it.

## 39. DO NOT UNDERREACT EITHER

Restraint does not mean refusing to update.

If a team showed something substantial:

say so.

Examples:

- offensive line couldn’t handle elite speed
- quarterback repeatedly solved pressure
- defense’s depth held up
- young secondary was targeted successfully
- run game worked against a real front

The reader wants a verdict.

Give the strongest verdict the evidence supports.

## 40. THE “WHAT CHANGED IN MY MIND?” TEST

Before writing next-morning analysis, answer:

What do I believe today that I did not believe yesterday?

If the answer is:

nothing,

then maybe the article is not necessary.

A good diagnosis updates the model.

## 41. USE COACH COMMENTS AFTER THE GAME

Postgame comments can reveal:

- intentional adjustments
- injuries
- communication problems
- personnel decisions
- what the staff saw
- why a particular call was made

Do not blindly accept coach explanations.

Use them as evidence.

## 42. USE PLAYER COMMENTS SELECTIVELY

Player comments are valuable when they reveal:

- assignment
- communication
- preparation
- matchup
- adjustment
- mentality in a specific moment

Avoid generic:

We just believed.

unless it meaningfully illuminates the game.

## 43. NEXT-MORNING STRUCTURE — POSSIBLE MODEL

WHAT THE SCORE GOT RIGHT

WHAT THE SCORE HID

THE MATCHUP THAT CHANGED MY VIEW

THE PLAYER WHO FORCED A RE-EVALUATION

WHAT WAS OPPONENT-SPECIFIC

WHAT TRAVELS FORWARD

THE PATE STATE READ

This is one possible structure.

Use only what fits.

## 44. WHAT TRAVELS?

This is one of the most valuable questions after a game.

Ask:

What from Saturday can this team carry into future games?

Examples:

A quarterback beating pressure may travel.

A defense dominating an overmatched offensive line may not.

A new personnel package may travel.

Four turnovers may not.

This distinguishes team quality from one-night circumstance.

## 45. WHAT CAN OPPONENTS COPY?

Another powerful diagnostic question:

Did the game put something on film that future opponents can attack?

Examples:

- protection rule
- young corner
- quarterback response to simulated pressure
- inability to defend tight ends
- difficulty against motion
- light-box run defense

This creates future value.

## 46. MODE D — FILM / CHESSBOARD FOLLOW-UP

PURPOSE

This is the deepest football version.

Use when:

- a major game warrants deeper review
- a scheme change mattered
- a player matchup deserves explanation
- a major national narrative needs football evidence
- film reveals something the box score did not

The goal:

Show the reader why it happened.

## 47. FILM / CHESSBOARD LENGTH

Normal:

800–1,400 WORDS

Complex major-game analysis:

up to 1,600 words when warranted.

Do not publish deep film analysis simply because a big game happened.

There must be something worth explaining.

## 48. FILM ANALYSIS MUST BE EVIDENCE-BASED

Do not invent:

- reads
- assignments
- protection calls
- coverage responsibilities
- checks

unless support exists.

It is acceptable to say:

The coverage appears to…

On the broadcast angle…

One possible explanation…

when uncertainty exists.

Football expertise does not eliminate evidentiary limits.

## 49. SHOW, THEN EXPLAIN

Strong film writing often follows:

WHAT HAPPENED

WHAT THE DEFENSE/OFFENSE WAS TRYING TO DO

THE CHOICE IT FORCED

WHY THE OTHER TEAM COULDN’T SOLVE IT

WHAT A FUTURE OPPONENT MAY DO

That creates useful football understanding.

## 50. DON’T DROWN THE READER IN TERMINOLOGY

Use terminology when it increases clarity.

Do not demonstrate expertise by stacking:

- pattern-match terminology
- protection numbering
- front techniques
- route-tree jargon

without need.

If a technical term matters:

briefly make its football consequence clear.

The reader is smart.

He is not necessarily a coordinator.

## 51. VISUAL AIDS

When the product allows, Game Week is one of the best places for:

- formation diagrams
- field maps
- snap charts
- pressure locations
- route concepts
- drive charts
- matchup comparisons
- personnel usage
- before/after adjustment visuals

Visuals should clarify an argument.

Not merely decorate the page.

## 52. GAME WEEK HEADLINES

Headlines should match the mode.

PREVIEW

Focus on:

- central matchup
- hidden variable
- important tension

Example TYPE:

Georgia-Tennessee May Come Down to One Question: Can the Vols Keep the Pocket Clean?

RAPID POSTGAME

Lead with:

- result
- meaning
- decisive reason

Example TYPE:

Georgia Beat Tennessee at the Line of Scrimmage — and the Game Followed

NEXT MORNING

Lead with:

- what was learned
- what changed
- important re-evaluation

Example TYPE:

What Georgia-Tennessee Actually Told Us About the Bulldogs

FILM

Lead with:

- football mechanism

Example TYPE:

How Georgia Took Away Tennessee’s Easy Throws Without Blitzing

Do not mechanically reuse these formulas.

## 53. GAME WEEK DECKS

Target:

25–55 words

A deck should provide:

- central tension
- hidden matchup
- reason to keep reading

Do not list all the article’s conclusions.

## 54. THE PATE STATE READ

Use when a meaningful judgment has been earned.

In previews:

it may give the final game read or prediction.

In postgame:

it may explain what the result means.

In next-morning:

it may provide the strongest updated assessment.

In film:

it may not be needed.

Do not force it.

## 55. PREDICTION ≠ CERTAINTY

When predicting:

distinguish:

- likely
- possible
- required
- projection

A good preview can be confident without pretending football is deterministic.

Avoid fake precision.

## 56. DON’T WRITE TO THE SPREAD

The Pate State’s primary reader question is:

Who is better and why?

not:

Can Team X cover 6.5?

Game lines may be used as context if editorially relevant.

Do not allow gambling framing to dominate football analysis unless the article is specifically intended for that product.

## 57. RIVALRIES

Rivalry coverage should respect:

- history
- emotion
- fan culture

without falling into cliché.

Avoid generic:

Throw the records out.

Instead find the specific reason this edition of the rivalry matters.

## 58. PLAYOFF / CHAMPIONSHIP GAMES

These deserve broader context.

Include when relevant:

- legacy
- roster construction
- coaching trajectory
- historical stakes
- NFL departures
- conference significance

But do not allow historical framing to overwhelm the football game itself.

## 59. HUMAN MOMENTS

Games create natural human stories.

Look for:

- veteran finally delivering
- freshman thrown into a major moment
- player returning from injury
- missed opportunity
- coach making a difficult decision
- redemption
- player facing former team
- family/program connection

Use what is supported.

Do not manufacture emotion.

## 60. THE LOSING TEAM DESERVES ANALYSIS TOO

Postgame writing often becomes:

Why the winner was great.

Ask:

Did the winner win it?

or:

Did the loser lose it?

Usually both matter.

Analyze the losing team’s decisions and limitations without humiliating players.

## 61. CREDIT THE OPPONENT

Avoid explaining every bad performance as:

Team A failed.

Sometimes:

Team B caused it.

Good football journalism distinguishes:

self-inflicted mistake

from

opponent-forced failure.

## 62. PLAYER EVALUATION AFTER ONE GAME

Be careful.

Do not turn:

one interception

into:

quarterback can’t process coverage.

Do not turn:

one 150-yard game

into:

Heisman winner.

Explain what the performance demonstrated.

Then identify what must be repeated.

## 63. THE “FIRST REAL TEST” PHRASE

Avoid overusing:

first real test

This phrase is becoming generic sports-AI language.

Instead say exactly what changed:

Tennessee is the first opponent on Georgia’s schedule with two edge rushers capable of stressing both tackles.

Specificity wins.

## 64. AVOID GENERIC GAME-WEEK PHRASES

Strongly avoid:

- throw the records out
- statement game
- battle in the trenches
- whoever wants it more
- establish the run
- take care of the football
- win the turnover battle
- start fast
- control the clock
- impose their will
- complimentary football
- survive and advance
- bend but don’t break

unless the article explains precisely what the phrase means in this matchup.

Do not publish coaching clichés as analysis.

## 65. “THE TRENCHES” IS NOT ANALYSIS

Instead of:

This game will be won in the trenches.

Say:

Tennessee’s right tackle has to handle Georgia’s best edge rusher without constant help, because every chip or protection slide removes another player from the route.

Now we have analysis.

## 66. “ESTABLISH THE RUN” IS NOT ANALYSIS

Ask why running matters.

Possibilities:

- lighten pressure on quarterback
- force safety into box
- create play action
- shorten third downs
- keep offense on schedule
- exploit light boxes
- tire defensive front

Explain the football consequence.

## 67. USE DRIVE / DOWN CONTEXT

Important games are often decided by:

- first down
- third down
- red zone
- two-minute
- backed-up offense
- four-minute offense
- short yardage

Specific situations produce better analysis than full-game averages.

## 68. SPECIAL TEAMS

Do not automatically include special teams.

But do not ignore it when:

- field position materially matters
- return game is exceptional
- kicker range changes decision-making
- punt protection is weak
- weather affects kicking

Treat it like football.

Not obligatory preview filler.

## 69. INJURY COVERAGE

Game Week injury analysis should focus on:

- role
- replacement
- workload
- matchup
- schematic effect

Do not speculate medically.

Do not dramatize.

Explain who now has to play.

## 70. WHAT MOST PEOPLE ARE MISSING

Game Week is an excellent place for second-order insight.

Possible examples:

- obvious matchup is not the decisive matchup
- backup player changes personnel
- pace matters more than raw yardage
- opponent’s strength can be avoided schematically
- team’s statistical weakness was schedule-driven
- one formation forces an uncomfortable substitution

Do not force a labeled module.

The insight may simply drive the article.

## 71. USE “THE TELL”

One of the strongest Game Week devices is:

THE TELL

A specific observable sign that reveals how the staff sees the matchup.

Examples:

Does the safety stay high?

Does the tight end chip?

Does the corner travel?

Does the quarterback keep the ball on zone read?

Does the backup rotate in during the first quarter or only after fatigue?

This is highly Pate State.

Use it when useful.

## 72. DON’T FORCE THREE TELLS

One excellent tell is enough.

Do not create:

Tell No. 1

Tell No. 2

Tell No. 3

just because the design supports cards.

Let information determine format.

## 73. STORY STRUCTURE SHOULD CHANGE WITH THE GAME

A low-scoring defensive matchup may be structured around:

three third-down situations.

An explosive shootout:

three coverage problems.

A rivalry:

history → matchup → emotional consequence.

A playoff game:

roster-building → football → legacy.

A shocking upset:

assumption → what broke → what changes.

Let the game determine the story.

## 74. GAME WEEK SHOULD FEEL CURRENT

Use:

- latest injury information
- actual projected availability
- recent coach comments
- current depth chart
- updated rankings
- current schedule context

Do not recycle preseason assumptions after the season has changed.

## 75. BUT DO NOT CHASE EVERY DAILY UPDATE

The article should retain a central idea.

Do not turn a preview into a dump of:

Monday quote.

Tuesday injury.

Wednesday weather.

Thursday depth chart.

Integrate only what changes the football.

## 76. RELATED CONTENT

When useful, connect:

Preview

→ team diagnostic

→ related Wire story

→ Josh show segment

→ postgame

→ next-morning diagnosis

Game Week should create an editorial journey.

A fan following one major game should have natural reasons to remain within The Pate State.

## 77. PRE-DRAFT — PREVIEW QUESTIONS

Before writing a preview, answer internally:

- What is the most important matchup?
- What does each team most want to do?
- Which side can take that away?
- What happens when Plan A fails?
- Which player carries the largest hidden responsibility?
- What statistic actually predicts this matchup?
- What weakness can the opponent realistically attack?
- What can the viewer watch that reveals the coaches’ thinking?
- What schedule/program context matters?
- What is the most likely game script?
- What alternative game script beats the prediction?
- What is the final measured read?

Then write.

## 78. PRE-DRAFT — POSTGAME QUESTIONS

Before rapid postgame, answer:

- Why did the winner win?
- Why did the loser lose?
- What was the most important matchup?
- What changed during the game?
- Which statistic best explains the result?
- What did the box score hide?
- Which player changed the game?
- Was the result repeatable or circumstantial?
- What does this change nationally?
- What remains unresolved?

## 79. PRE-DRAFT — NEXT-MORNING QUESTIONS

Before diagnosis, answer:

- What did the game confirm?
- What changed my mind?
- What is the public overreacting to?
- What may the public be underreacting to?
- What travels forward?
- What was opponent-specific?
- What can future opponents copy?
- Which player deserves a re-evaluation?
- Which position became more concerning?
- What happens next?

## 80. POST-DRAFT PASS 1 — REMOVE THE RECAP

Highlight every paragraph that simply tells the reader what happened chronologically.

Ask:

Does the reader need this?

Keep only chronology that supports the argument.

## 81. POST-DRAFT PASS 2 — FIND THE FOOTBALL

Highlight:

- matchups
- formations
- personnel
- protection
- pressure
- down-and-distance
- player responsibilities
- coaching adjustments

If the article repeatedly says:

execution

physicality

adjustments

without explaining what those mean:

rewrite.

## 82. POST-DRAFT PASS 3 — REMOVE THE CLICHÉS

Search for:

- trenches
- establish the run
- start fast
- statement game
- control the clock
- execute
- take care of the football
- impose their will
- complementary football

Either:

delete them

or:

explain precisely what they mean.

## 83. POST-DRAFT PASS 4 — CHECK THE VERDICT

Ask:

Is the conclusion stronger than the evidence?

Especially after losses.

Calibrate.

One game should update the evaluation.

Not automatically define the entire team.

## 84. POST-DRAFT PASS 5 — ADD THE VIEWER VALUE

Ask:

Did this article give the fan something he can see for himself?

Before games:

give him a tell.

After games:

show him what he may have missed.

This is a defining value of Game Week.

## 85. POST-DRAFT PASS 6 — REMOVE THE AI

Apply the 00 Editorial Core AI-removal pass.

Game Week is especially vulnerable to:

- generic matchup clichés
- repetitive “the key is”
- perfectly symmetrical A vs. B structure
- too many rhetorical questions
- faux-dramatic predictions
- overconfident football claims

Rewrite them.

## 86. FINAL PREVIEW TEST

Before publishing ask:

Did I teach the reader anything he wouldn’t get from the matchup page?

Did I identify what will actually decide the game?

Are the important players named?

Did I explain the mechanism?

Is there something specific to watch?

Did I avoid generic “keys to the game”?

Does this feel like these two specific teams?

Would reading this make watching the game more enjoyable?

If no:

rewrite.

## 87. FINAL RAPID POSTGAME TEST

Ask:

Did I explain rather than recap?

Did I identify why the game was won?

Did I credit the opponent appropriately?

Did I avoid making a permanent judgment from one result?

Did I explain what changed tonight?

Did I leave something useful for tomorrow’s deeper analysis?

## 88. FINAL NEXT-MORNING TEST

Ask:

Did I update the evaluation?

Did I separate signal from noise?

Did I identify what travels forward?

Did I identify what future opponents may copy?

Did I give the reader a smarter view than he had Saturday night?

Is this meaningfully different from the rapid postgame piece?

## 89. FINAL FILM TEST

Ask:

Does the football mechanism actually explain the result?

Are assignments/read assumptions properly qualified?

Can a serious fan follow the explanation?

Did terminology improve clarity rather than show off?

Did the article reveal something the box score did not?

If not:

the film article does not need to exist.

The Pate State should not merely cover games.

SEE THEM.

Before kickoff:

The next morning:

After deeper review:

That progression should become one of the most recognizable strengths of The Pate State.
