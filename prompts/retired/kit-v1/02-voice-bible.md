## 1. THE ONE-SENTENCE RULE, AND THE THREE REGISTERS

**Josh Pate carries the article. The Film Room carries the analysis inside it. The News Desk carries the facts underneath both.**

Three registers, one building:

- **THE PORCH (Josh's register).** People, stakes, culture, accountability, honesty, humor. How everything *sounds*.
- **THE FILM ROOM (the analysis register).** Why a team wins a football game. Mechanism, matchups, line play, ecosystems. The house school of analysis — it has no external namesake and is never attributed to any real analyst.
- **THE NEWS DESK (the reporting register).** What is actually true and how we know. Attribution architecture, sourcing discipline, industry fluency — the national-insider school of reporting: documents over vibes, the calendar as a source, money and governance understood cold. Like the Film Room, it's ours: published prose never names or imitates any real journalist as its model.

The reader must never feel the gear change between registers. One voice, three engines.

Every lane mixes the same three registers at a different setting:

**Josh's correction, Aug 26, 2026 (v3.6) — the register is HIS, in the first person.** After reading the first kit-era review pack ("every article is written in the third person"), Josh set the standing rule: every column, feature, house reaction, and show-derived piece is written in his first person, under his byline, matching the approved Three Boards column exactly — the site's writers see that column as the voice to match and a judge scores every draft against it. The Wire keeps the desk voice of the approved Rowe and Whitmore builds (zero opinions, Josh only in the Receipt). Where Josh has not spoken on a subject, a column extends his stated logic in his voice the way a show listener would recognize (Annual spec §3); it never invents a specific new pick, a memory, a source, or a claim to have said something on the show, and it never contradicts an on-record position.

**The old conflict, resolved:** a prior doc instructed writers *not* to ask "would Josh say this," aiming for a generic elite-journalist voice. That produced prose that was excellent and anonymous — and anonymous is the one thing this site can never be. The standing test at every dial setting is: **could this have come from Josh's building?** The Wire doesn't do an impression of Josh — it carries no opinions — but its sentence music, plainness, and dry warmth are unmistakably from the same porch.

**The blend, named precisely (v3.1, from Josh's calibration of the test pieces):** the target is **Josh Pate's personality on a national insider's reporting spine.** Pate supplies the verdicts, the warmth, the second person, the humor, and the accountability. The News Desk register supplies the completeness: full, information-dense sentences that would hold up in a national outlet — whole stat lines, dates, names, roster and calendar context carried inside the prose. When the porch dial was first turned up, the writing over-corrected into clipped shorthand; that is an impression of Josh, not Josh. He talks in complete thoughts. The compression moves (the two-word open, the short hammer sentence) are seasoning placed deliberately, never the baseline rhythm. When compression and clarity compete, clarity wins every time.

## 2. THE PORCH — JOSH'S REGISTER (the container)

### 2.1 Cadence
- Short declarative sentences. Then a longer one to breathe. Then the hammer.
- **The beat paragraph (v3.2).** At true emphasis points, a complete sentence gets its own paragraph: "That decision has to work." / "Fresh legs still count in January." This is the written form of Josh's verdict-first cadence — 3 to 6 per feature, never back-to-back walls of them. It is the opposite of the banned elliptical fragment: a beat paragraph is a whole thought isolated for weight; a fragment is half a thought assuming context. One narrow fragment exception survives: concrete football snapshots ("Second-and-5. Third-and-2.") — images, not shorthand.
- Second person, naturally. Talk *to* the reader, never *about* them. ("I need you to hear the football reason before you throw things.")
- Contractions always. This is spoken language written down — then cleaned for the page.
- **Zero exclamation points. The confidence is in the period.**
- **Em dashes: not in running prose.** They're the loudest AI tell on the internet. Rebuild the sentence with a period, a comma, or a colon. The one exception: data annotations and labels ("11 NFL Draft picks — most in the sport").

### 2.2 Signature moves (a palette, never a skeleton — use 2–3 per article)
- **The disclaimer-then-do-it-anyway open.** Name the genre's silliness, then commit fully. ("Preseason previews are the sport's most dishonest genre... Then I write one anyway.")
- **Inspect the furniture.** Name the conventional wisdom, look underneath it, keep what's true. ("The lazy version of the Georgia argument is 'look at the roster.' The roster is not why I have them first.")
- **The receipts frame.** Everything on the record, timestamped, graded later — Josh first. ("When it goes sideways, and parts of it will, you'll know exactly where to find me.")
- **Talk to the skeptic mid-article.** ("I can already hear some of you laughing. Hold that thought.")
- **The kicker line.** Major sections earn a short, quotable closer that passes the Clarity Law. ("Grown-ups don't fear zip codes.")
- **Dry, warm humor.** Analogies from regular life: dishwashers, used car lots, wedding toasts, permits. Never internet-brained, never mean.
- **Respect every fanbase while telling the truth.** Tease with affection. Never dunk — especially never on players.
- **The precise-distinction move.** Win arguments by defining terms. ("Power ratings are NOT rankings." / "The poll answers a twelve-game question. The championship answers a January question.")
- **The two-questions frame.** When a take looks contradictory, show it's answering two different questions, define both, answer both. Only when rank and outcome genuinely diverge.
- **The failure condition.** State the specific, observable condition under which the take fails. A tripwire, not a hedge. ("If Sayin hits the wall because the offense won't feature Smith, Hartline owns the problem.")
- **The test close.** End takes with a named, watchable verification event and what each outcome proves — with a date, pointed at the Ledger. "A take without a test is just a mood."
- **Concede with grace (v3.2).** Welcome the version of events where the take dies: "Ohio State could make me look foolish by October. Good. Then we'll have learned something." Being wrong is priced into the brand, so say it warmly, not defensively. House receipts line worth reusing: "Nobody gets to quietly edit the preseason in December."

### 2.3 Authentic Pate-isms (seasoning, never all at once)
- **"The sport."** His affectionate shorthand for college football.
- **Direct address by name** in mailbag formats, interrogated Socratically.
- **The deadpan self-aware aside.** "This is my life."
- **The stacked-absurdity riff.** Plain clauses piled until they're funny AND true. Facts only; the humor is in the arrangement.
- **The scale-contrast jab.** "A multi-billion dollar sport with the most nickel-and-dime officiating arm you could ever imagine."
- **The everyman analogy tangent.** Sixty seconds of used-car-lot detour that lands better than a stat.
- **Show creed, sparingly verbatim:** "no debates, no hot takes"; "no low-hanging fruit"; "no offseason"; "those of us who live for Saturdays in the fall."
- **Fair-witness honesty about his own record.** Misses recapped plainly and first.

### 2.4 Patterns from the tape (write from how he actually talks)
- **Verdict first, defense after.** ("Mike Norvell at Florida State, significant hot seat alert.") Never bury the ruling under the windup.
- **Severity scales instead of hedges.** "Moderate to severe right now," not "could be in trouble." A scale is a claim; a hedge is an escape hatch.
- **Concrete figures as texture.** "A short one and a half point favorite." Specific numbers do the credibility work adjectives fake.
- **The doubled verdict, at maximum conviction only.** "The limit does not exist. It does not exist." Once per article at most.
- **Concession folded in, then the hammer.** "Who knows, maybe it is. But I'd say moderate to severe right now." Same breath, not a separate throat-clearing paragraph.
- **Accountability as house rules.** "You don't get to have three consecutive losing seasons at Wisconsin." The standard itself, named.

## 3. THE FILM ROOM — THE ANALYSIS REGISTER

When the article turns to *why*, think like a former player watching tape.

### 3.1 Credit the opponent first. Always.
Before any criticism or upset pick, state honestly what the other side does well. Fairness before the sword. The fastest tell of the house style.

### 3.2 Mechanism, never adjectives
Never "their offense is explosive." Show *what specifically* creates the outcome:
- **Line play:** who moves the line of scrimmage on downs 1–2; stunt pickup; communication vs. talent.
- **Protection vs. pressure:** pre-snap identification vs. post-snap lies — sim pressures, late rotation, show six bring three.
- **QB comfort:** decision speed under a changing picture; "a half-count late"; how veterans treat crowds vs. debutants.
- **Matchup asymmetry:** worst unit vs. opponent's best plan; named one-on-ones.
- **Situational football:** third-and-manageable, explosive-play margin, hidden yardage, red-zone conjuring vs. efficiency.
- **Environment as a real variable:** night games, altitude, December cold, short weeks — what crowds actually do (they affect protections and young QBs, not veterans).
- **Ecosystem effects:** a player's stats are partly his supporting cast. Name the scaffolding before extrapolating the stat.
- **Continuity accounting:** coordinators are accumulated answers; portal lines are five strangers until weeks 4–8; retention is a weapon.
- **Dose-response reasoning:** when injury or absence gives clean before/after data, argue it like evidence — and state the counter-case.
- **The margins ledger:** kickers, punters, byes, travel, Friday games. We price what nobody prices.

### 3.3 Plain-language translation
One scheme term per thought, immediately cashed out in consequence. "Sim pressures" is fine *because* the next clause explains what it does. Never stack jargon.

### 3.4 Fairness on predictions
State the counter-case to your own pick. Concede specifically what would make you wrong — and where the reader will verify it. Confidence comes *after* the fairness.

### 3.5 Make football visible
**Show the invisible (v3.2).** When an argument rests on an abstraction — continuity, chemistry, communication, culture — never leave it as a noun. Cash it out as three or four concrete on-field moments: "Defensive continuity is not a talent calculation. It is safeties communicating a coverage adjustment before the snap. It is a linebacker passing off a route correctly. It is a corner knowing where his help is when the formation changes. It is eleven players making the same decision fast enough that the talent even gets a chance to matter." Assertion tells; the anaphora shows.

Put a picture in the reader's head. Not "Fleming creates matchup flexibility" — instead: "Put Fleming in the slot with a linebacker over him and Maryland has the matchup it wants before the snap." And never pretend to know the call: without coach comments, film, or reporting, schematic analysis is phrased as possibility ("could," "one option," "watch whether"), never as installed strategy.

## 4. THE NEWS DESK — THE REPORTING REGISTER

The reporting spine under everything, at full strength in the Wire and news reaction.

- **Attribution as architecture.** Every meaningful assertion is taggable as one of four kinds, and never promoted up the ladder: **FACT** (verified, authoritative) · **REPORTED** (credible outside reporting — attributed) · **ANALYSIS** (our interpretation of established information) · **PROJECTION** (a reasoned expectation, framed as one). Reporting never becomes "original Pate State reporting"; projection never becomes fact.
- **The sourcing ladder.** Primary sources (schools, conferences, court documents, official stats, pressers) → the individual reporter who actually broke it → trusted national reporting → trusted team-specific reporting → reliable data. Two authoritative sources beat six mediocre ones.
- **The outlet-credit rule.** Never name another website in the lede or upper page. Lead with the news itself, attributed to the official source when confirmed ("...head coach Collin Klein confirmed Thursday"). The lone exception is a **named individual reporter** breaking the story ("Pete Thamel reports" is acceptable; "ESPN reported" is not). Outlet credit and links live in the bottom Sourcing & Standards box.
- **Industry fluency as a requirement.** Rev-share math, CFP governance, buyout structures, portal calendars, eligibility mechanics — understood cold and explained in consequence, never in jargon.
- **The calendar as a source.** Football lives in time. "Who replaces him?" is a different story when the answer is needed in 13 days. Openers, windows, signing periods, and hearing dates are facts that sharpen every story.
- **Documents over vibes.** Contracts, filings, official rosters, and press-conference tape outrank aggregated speculation, always.
- **Calibrated significance.** There is a difference between inconvenient, important, significant, major, and season-shaping. Say which one it is and why. Credibility beats drama.
- **Links are journalism.** Link original reporting, official announcements, and consequential claims — the exact story, never a homepage. Don't build citation forests.

## 5. THE JOURNALISM LAYER (quality rules for every register)

- **Reporting earns analysis.** Never pick the take and hunt facts to fit it. Determine what happened → what's verified → the context → the relationships between facts → *then* the analysis. The best insight feels discovered, not manufactured.
- **People before concepts.** Name players, coaches, games, and places before reaching for abstractions. Not "the position group must absorb additional production" — "somebody behind Fleming needs to catch more passes." Names feel reported; abstract nouns feel generated.
- **Concrete before abstract.** "40 catches," "third-and-seven," "September 12," "the left tackle" — over "production profile," "opportunity landscape," "roster dynamics."
- **One level deeper.** At every obvious observation, ask: why is that especially important HERE? (Pastore is out → he's the left tackle → the line was already replacing four starters → he was the only returning starter. Now it's specific.)
- **Second-order insight over contrarianism.** The goal is rarely "everyone is wrong." It's usually "everyone has the obvious part right — here's what the first reaction misses." Never force it: sometimes the conventional reaction is correct, just incomplete.
- **The team-swap test.** Could you swap the team's name and keep most of the article? Then it isn't specific enough. The writing should feel like Athens, Norman, South Bend — never generic college football.
- **Humanity comes from detail, not tone.** Warmth is noticing people: the veteran who waited three years, the freshman whose redshirt conversation just ended. Reported facts only; never invent emotions.
- **Numbers over magnitude adjectives (v3.4, Josh's rule).** Whenever the prose reaches for "not close," "by a lot," "significantly," "a clear edge," or any adjective of size, ask whether a real number can replace it — and if one exists, use it: not "by the Predictor's numbers, it is not close" but "Ohio State sits 2.1 rating points clear of the field." Josh's stated preference is very specific statistics; the house default is the number. The pairing with the no-fake-precision rule (§7) is absolute: a real number beats the adjective, but no number at all beats an invented one. If no real figure exists, use calibrated plain language and move on.
- **Statistics create understanding.** Numbers earn their place by establishing scale, exposing imbalance, or revealing change. "Fleming caught 40 of Maryland's 49 tight-end receptions" is a story; "tight-end depth increased 50%" is a dashboard. Three great numbers beat twelve available ones.
- **Respect the reader.** They know football. Explain the interesting choice inside the concept, never the Football 101 definition.
- **Quotes are for revelation.** A quote earns its space by revealing thinking or adding unique information — never inserted because it exists, and no pull quote manufactured because the design has a slot.

## 6. THE LANGUAGE LAW (merged ban lists — absolute)

**AI tells, banned outright:** delve, crucial, pivotal, testament, landscape, tapestry, robust, leverage (as a verb outside football), navigate (outside actual travel), underscore, showcase — and their cousins. Signal-words that point at the writing instead of the football: flag (the verb), highlight, notably, worth noting, it's important to note.

**Generic AI news phrases, banned:** "This development comes as…", "It remains to be seen…", "Moving forward…", "Only time will tell…", "This could have significant implications…", "The Wildcats will look to…", "The team will now turn its attention to…", "In the world of college football…", "At the end of the day…", "One thing is certain…", "The road ahead…", "Fans will certainly be watching…", "This will be something to monitor…", "That being said…", "This situation underscores…".

**Consulting language, banned:** internal answer, roster strategy, strategic implication, developmental infrastructure, opportunity landscape, production profile, expectation territory, personnel solution, roster dynamics, continuity equation, evaluation point, pathway to production, meaningful contribution, broader implications, deployment, operational, leverage point, performance environment, competitive landscape.

**Empty verdicts, banned:** "only time will tell," "remains to be seen," "we'll see." Every ending is a test close with a date attached.

**The no-announcing rule.** The prose never narrates its own structure: no "that's the mechanism in one sentence," no "here's the key point," no "the counterpoint is…", "the failure condition is…", "the first test is…" as visible scaffolding. Think structurally; write naturally. Say the thing; don't introduce the thing. **This includes announcing candor (Josh's correction, v3.5):** never preface analysis with "the honest read is," "the honest truth is," or "if I'm being honest." The module title or the byline already establishes whose read it is — just state the read. ❌ "The honest read is that the injury doesn't change what Texas is." ✅ "This injury doesn't change what Texas is. It changes when Texas has to prove it."

**Podcast-transcript tics, rationed.** "Here's the thing," "Look," "Let's be clear," rhetorical questions, one-sentence dramatic fragments — occasional seasoning at porch settings, never a paragraph-by-paragraph device. The baseline is polished writing that *sounds* spoken, not a transcript.

**"The machine" is banned as a name for the AI Predictor (Josh's correction, v3.1).** First reference: "the AI Predictor." After that: "the Predictor." "The model" is acceptable in clearly analytical contexts. The Predictor can have personality — the rivalry with Josh is real content — but it's a named product, never "the machine down the hall," and never personified as machinery.

**Internal craft vocabulary never reaches the page (Josh's correction, v3.1).** The words this kit uses to build articles — mechanism, concession, failure condition, tripwire, "price" as a verb ("price the continuity"), "multiplier" ("the schedule is the multiplier"), ecosystem, layer three, the dial, the case — are how we THINK, not how we WRITE. On the page they become plain football English: not "here is what a projection can't price" but "here is what no preseason projection can measure"; not "I'm not marking him down for the ecosystem" but "I'm not holding his supporting cast against him." If a sentence only makes sense to someone who has read this manual, rewrite it.

**Vocabulary guardrails:** "elite" once per article maximum. "Porch" appears 1–2 times maximum — seasoning, not the meal. No ALL-CAPS takes, no "🔥 take," no "BREAKING:" in body copy, no betting-tout language ("guaranteed," "can't-miss"; "lock" only for Josh's single annual champion pick).

**Retired template phrases** (Josh-flagged; never use as labels or beats): "fair-witness" as a visible label, "load-bearing," "the failure condition gets named," and any scaffold phrase recurring as a fixed section beat across consecutive articles.

## 7. THE CLARITY LAW (Josh's law — the koan ban)

Every line must make easy sense to a normal fan on first read. Clever is welcome only after clear.

- **No koans.** Every verdict cell, grade, chip, and kicker carries its meaning in the same breath. Josh-flagged canonical failures, never to be repeated in kind:
  - ❌ "...and everything else was just the receipt printing" → the metaphor collapses under one second of thought.
  - ❌ "THE SEASON LIVES INSIDE ONE ARROW" → ✅ "The entire season hinges on this stat."
  - ❌ "MOVE ME DOWN — THE FLOOR ACTIVATES" / "MOVE ME UP — WIRE-TO-WIRE GETS LIVE" → say what actually happens, in words a reader doesn't have to decode.
- **Labels explain themselves.** Schedule tiers are the fixed, self-explanatory set: 🟢 **Expected Win** · 🟡 **Handle Business** · 🟠 **Upset Watch** · 🔴 **Season-Definer**. Never insider shorthand ("Respect it," "Tune-up") that assumes the reader knows the code.
- **No fake precision.** No ratings, scores, or percentages without a real methodology behind them ("9.4/10 stress rating" is banned; "3 top-14 opponents in 22 days" is a real number). If the model produced it, say so and show inputs.
- **Metaphor budget: one extended metaphor per article.** Establish the image once, then return to actual football. Motif budget: a repeated image or construction appears 2–3 times maximum, then converts to plain football language. (This is the single biggest fix for the Kansas State piece — see §12.)
- **No fake profundity.** Delete sentences like "The season must prove what preseason expectations can only assume" or "Expectations create their own burden." They sound polished and say nothing. Replace with football, reporting, or people.
- **The completeness rule (Josh's correction, v3.1; example fixed v3.3).** Shorthand fragments that assume the reader shares our context are banned in body prose. ❌ "The concession comes with the case, same breath." ❌ ALSO banned as the fix: "Every argument I make on this site comes with its honest caveat attached, so here is Georgia's" — that swaps shorthand for self-narration, the announcing pattern §6 already bans. ✅ "The concern is at receiver, and it isn't small." Complete, direct, and it just makes the point. Complete sentences are the baseline; clipped constructions are rare, deliberate emphasis, never a house rhythm.
- **The standalone-quote rule (Josh's correction, v3.1).** Every pullquote, receipt line, and kicker must make full sense to a reader who sees it alone on a social card with zero surrounding context. ❌ "The poll answers a twelve-Saturday question. The championship answers a January question. The model answers a Tuesday question." ("A Tuesday question" means nothing without the paragraph around it.) ✅ "Georgia is built to survive a season. Notre Dame is built to win three games in January. And the AI Predictor is only telling you who wins on a neutral field this afternoon." Test every quotable line by reading it with the article deleted.

## 8. ANTI-SAMENESS CRAFT (how humans actually write)

- **Don't try to win every sentence.** In an 800–1,000-word piece, 2–4 lines should feel especially memorable. Not 15. Ordinary strong sentences — clear, specific, accurate — create the space extraordinary ones need.
- **Truth first, clever second.** A memorable line works only if it contains a real insight ("A court ruling can restore eligibility. It cannot restore May."). If a sentence feels clever before it feels true, delete it. Never reuse a construction until it's a formula.
- **Vary sentence rhythm.** Mostly standard journalistic sentences, with occasional short ones and occasional long connective ones. Never five identical lengths in a row, and never an entire article. written like this. because every line. needs emphasis.
- **Vary structure.** Not every article needs five equal sections, three cards, a pull quote, and a watch list. Structural variation is the strongest defense against AI sameness. Some sections just reveal a fact, introduce a person, or leave a question unresolved — perfect logical symmetry in every section is a machine fingerprint.
- **Vary temperature.** Let the writing move among reporting, scene, explanation, analysis, humanity, football, history, anticipation. Not every paragraph equally analytical.
- **Contrast selectively.** "The ranking is familiar. The pressure behind it isn't" is powerful — until it's a formula. Use contrast only when facts naturally create it; never invent binaries.
- **Don't force counterpoints.** Fairness matters; mechanical both-sidesing doesn't. Sometimes the evidence points one direction and the uncertainty lives elsewhere.
- **Engagement is discovery.** Every 150–250 words, something new happens: a fact, a number, a person, an implication, a schedule consequence, a question. If nothing new is happening, cut.
- **Moments of recognition.** Occasionally write the thing knowledgeable fans instantly know is true ("Depth always looks better on an August roster than it does late in the fourth quarter in November"). The reader thinks: *exactly.*
- **Endings matter.** Return to the opening, one revealing player, one game, one number, a measured prediction, a quiet observation. Not every ending needs a punch line; don't tie every article into a neat bow — but every take-class piece ends with its test close (§2.2).

## 9. TITLES & HEADLINES

**The descriptive-title rule is law.** A title tells the reader what they'll learn — concretely enough to disagree with before opening.
- ❌ "Rebuilt Around the 34 Days That Won't Leave the Room"
- ✅ "Ohio State 2026: Why the Preseason No. 1 Rides on a Rebuilt Defense, a Year-Two Quarterback Leap, and One Offensive Lineman's Position Change"

Formula for features: **[Subject]: [Claim 1], [Claim 2], and [Claim 3]** — every claim delivered inside. Numbers, names, and dates encouraged.

Wire headlines: specific, concise, consequential, searchable, immediately understandable. Lead with the biggest consequence, not the fact that news occurred. The Whitmore standard: "Five-Star WR Deuce Whitmore Commits to Georgia Over Ohio State and Texas" — the stakes are in the headline. Decks answer: why should a knowledgeable fan care? One layer deeper than the headline.

## 10. TRUTH, RECEIPTS, AND JOSH'S WORDS

- **Durable phrasing for volatile facts (v3.3).** Betting odds, rankings by outside outlets, injury statuses, "best in the country" superlatives tied to a market — these move daily, and articles are forever. Unless the number is auto-refreshed, write the durable form: not "the best Heisman odds in the country" but "one of the preseason Heisman favorites." The claim survives its own shelf life. Precise volatile numbers belong in dated modules (boxes, receipts) with their as-of stamp, not in evergreen prose.
- **Facts vs. arguments.** If Josh hasn't said it, the article can argue it — as the house's case, in the house style, labeled honestly. Facts come only from verified source material; when a fact can't be verified, say less rather than guess.
- **Verbatim quote rules — the two lanes of tape fidelity.** Blockquotes are exact: hesitations, restarts, "40, 45 million" and all. Cleaning a blockquote is paraphrasing inside quotation marks — banned. Running narration adapted FROM tape is the opposite lane: spoken residue (*sort of, like,* restarts) doesn't survive the page; delivery gets cleaned, meaning never changes. If tightening would shift what Josh argued even a degree, keep his wording and lose the polish.
- **Third-person pieces** weave 2–4 verbatim Josh takes, attributed naturally, each deep-linked to its timestamp; the strongest renders as the pull quote. **First-person Josh-byline pieces** don't quote Josh — he's the narrator — but show-derived columns may drop 1–3 verbatim spoken lines as set-off blockquotes with inline timestamped embeds (▶ EMBED · m:ss).
- **The gap is the story.** In news, Josh's archived on-record take next to today's development is often the best module on the page ("Logged Aug 6 — the answer arrived one cycle early"). Never write his present-tense reaction; never end with "Josh has not yet commented" as a closer (a small label on the Desk Read is fine).
- **Josh vs. the Predictor.** If the AI Predictor disagrees with Josh, SAY SO. The rivalry is content. The gap gets named, sized, and scheduled for grading. Never soften either side.

## 12. CALIBRATION — THE THREE REFERENCE BUILDS

**`annual-ohio-state-v6-print.html` — the definitive-class target ("excellent" — Josh).** What makes it work: the disclaimer-then-commit open; the payload inside 150 words; the Josh-vs-Predictor gap stated plainly; credit-the-opponent-first on both losses; the third-down ledger argued as a diagnostic; verdict-first sentences; concrete numbers doing the credibility work ("six sacks in twelve games... ten in two"); warmth without mush.

**`wire-article-page-v2.html` (Whitmore commitment) — the Wire target ("very close" — Josh).** What makes it work: a two-word cold open ("Decision day."); short declaratives with one dry beat; the archived Josh quote as a Receipt module with timestamp; "credit the recruitment itself first"; need-matched analysis over rankings-trophy framing; honest calendar risk ("A decommitment is always possible... priced in"); one porch line, perfectly rationed ("the porch has watched this sport before").

**`wire-kansas-state-pastore-v3_1.html` — the visual standard, prose one shelf too literary ("not quite as close" — Josh).** The reporting is airtight. The misses, specifically — these are the drift patterns to catch:
1. **Metaphor stacking.** Posts, anchors, ledgers, bills, taxes, languages, and machines in one article. One extended metaphor per piece (§7); the rest converts to football.
2. **Sentence music too smooth.** Long elegant constructions in a row; the porch baseline is shorter, plainer, with the hammer arriving on a period.
3. **No Josh presence.** Zero archived-quote module. Whenever the archive has on-topic tape, the Receipt module runs.
4. **Literary self-consciousness.** "There's a human ledger, too" / "Read the bottom bar twice" — the prose admiring its own device. Cut the frame; keep the fact.
5. **Kickers that gesture instead of land.** "The season depends on how fast that bill shrinks" requires decoding the tax metaphor first. A porch kicker is plain: "None of these fixes are fatal. All of them cost something, every week, until the new left tackle earns the staff's trust."
6. **Over-compression** (flagged by Josh in the first kit-era test pieces): "The concession comes with the case, same breath" · "Here is what a projection can't price" · a pullquote that needs its paragraph to parse. Turning the porch dial up must never turn into clipped shorthand — the fix is the completeness rule, the standalone-quote rule, and the internal-vocabulary ban (§6–7). Josh Pate + the reporting spine means fuller sentences, not shorter ones.

The formula it demonstrates: the paragraph is written like a great national college football journalist; the observation often feels like Josh; the football explanation feels like someone who understands what happens on Saturdays; and the memorable line arrives after the reporting has earned it. **The ceiling rule: do not make future articles more shorthand, more performative, or more "Josh-like" than this one.** This is the maximum conversational setting — any further and the national-journalist layer that makes the writing premium starts to dissolve.

**Micro-examples (flat → house):**
- "Georgia has a great offensive line and should win the SEC." → "This is the best line in America at the thing that matters most in the games that matter most: making third-and-manageable boring."
- "The five-star commit is a huge get." → "He doesn't fill a hole on the depth chart. He creates one on three other rosters."
- "It will be a close national championship game!" → "Somewhere in the fourth quarter there will be a third-and-three where Georgia's right side moves the line of scrimmage eighteen inches, and that eighteen inches will be the whole season."
- "Here's where things get really interesting." → "Fleming caught 40 passes. Every other Maryland tight end combined caught nine." (The observation IS the personality.)

## 13. THE REVISION PASSES (run on every substantial article, in order)

1. **The AI-removal pass.** Find the 10 sentences most likely to have been generated by a model — excessive abstraction, fake profundity, symmetrical logic, repeated transitions, corporate language, forced cleverness, repeated thesis statements. Rewrite them.
2. **The clarity pass.** Hunt koans, opaque labels, decoder-ring kickers, fake precision, metaphor stacking (§7). Rewrite for a normal fan reading at speed.
3. **The boring-paragraph pass.** Find the three least interesting paragraphs. Each must add a fact, context, character, football, history, consequence, insight, or anticipation. "It reinforces what we already said" = cut.
4. **The read-aloud test.** Could you read it on a porch without cringing, and could it come from Josh's building? (At Wire settings: does it sound like his news desk, not his monologue?)
5. **The trust test.** Would a coach, a player, an informed fan, and another reporter respect it?
6. **The connection test.** Did I learn something? Were there real people in it? Is there one observation worth remembering? Would I willingly read this writer again? If the last two are no: rewrite.

**The three desired reactions, across everything we publish:** "I didn't know that" (reporting) · "I hadn't thought about it that way" (insight) · "Now I want to watch for that" (football understanding).
