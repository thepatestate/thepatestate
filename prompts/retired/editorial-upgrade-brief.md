# OBJECTIVE

Upgrade all written content on ThePateState.com from competent AI-assisted sports writing to A+ national-caliber college football journalism.

The goal is NOT longer, more verbose, more dramatic, or more complicated. The goal is that every story feels: intelligent, authoritative, highly readable, distinctly Pate State, specific, memorable, human-edited, useful even to someone who already watched the Josh Pate video, and structurally different from the article published before it.

**The standard: a reader would not assume the article was AI-generated from its prose or structure alone.**

Every story should leave readers with at least one of three reactions: "I didn't know that." / "I hadn't thought about it that way." / "I need to send this to somebody." If an article accomplishes none of those, it isn't ready to publish.

The Pate State should not be "AI that turns Josh Pate videos into articles." It should be **the smartest written extension of Josh Pate's college football universe** — Josh supplies the point of view, models, predictions, transcripts, recurring language, and audience; AI amplifies that advantage instead of flattening it into a template.

# PART 1 — WHAT'S WORKING (DO NOT BREAK THESE)

1. **Thesis-first structure.** Articles state the argument in the first two sentences instead of summarizing a video.
2. **Conditions, not hot takes.** The ceiling/floor/failure-condition *thinking* is more disciplined than most human CFB writing. Keep the thinking; fix the phrasing (Part 3).
3. **Receipts culture.** Quotes linked to the exact timestamp in the episode is a differentiator no competitor has. Keep and expand.
4. **Epistemic honesty.** "Expected to transfer," "reported, not confirmed," "projection — not a confirmed depth chart." The standards page's traceability and verbatim-quote requirements are excellent. Never lose this.
5. **The best pieces isolate a specific tension** — e.g., the JP Poll article distinguishing a predictive power rating from a résumé ranking, then giving LSU–Clemson as the test. That's real sports analysis.
6. **The expanded Wire format on big stories** (Castellanos, the Oklahoma/Venables piece): news → stakes → overlooked angle → analysis → what to watch. This is the A- benchmark. Its problems are repetition and metadata, not structure.
7. **Falsifiable closings** ("watch the first defense that keeps six in the box") — keep as one ending style among several (see Endings).

# PART 2 — TWO MANDATORY RULES (FIX IMMEDIATELY)

## RULE 1: The authorship/voice rule

Never mix a Pate State Staff byline with unexplained first-person Josh narration. Articles currently bylined "The Pate State Staff" say "**I** released a power rating…", "**I** expect Curtis to win the job…", "Darien Mensah is **my** Heisman pick… **I'm** 99.9% sure" — while quoting Josh Pate in third person in the same piece. This is the single largest credibility/AI tell on the site.

Two acceptable systems (use both — it creates editorial variety):

**JOSH PATE COLUMN** — If the article faithfully adapts Josh's argument in his voice: byline **Josh Pate**, label "Adapted from The Josh Pate Show." First person acceptable.

**PATE STATE STAFF** — Third person for Josh's opinions, always:
- ✅ "Pate expects Curtis to win the job." / "Pate's model projects Ole Miss at 9–3."
- ❌ "I expect Curtis to win the job." / "I have Ole Miss at 9–3."

Never let Staff become Josh halfway through an article. This rule is mandatory, sitewide, retroactive.

## RULE 2: Stop writing every story from the same template

Across Notre Dame, Ole Miss, Vanderbilt, Tennessee, Oklahoma, the chaos article, and the longform conversions, the same conceptual vocabulary and architecture repeats: ceiling / floor / margin / condition / failure condition / the real question / the test / "watch X for the answer" / three explanatory subheads / thesis restatement. Individually these are good analytical devices. Repeated across dozens of articles, they are an unmistakable AI fingerprint.

**Build a library of 10–12 article architectures and deliberately rotate them.** The content dictates the structure, never the reverse. Before drafting, consciously select the best architecture for THIS story — never default to the previous article's. Architectures to include:
1. The condition ladder (current default — now max ~1 in 5 articles)
2. Open with a surprising number, interrogate it
3. Open with a scene or moment from Josh's show, widen to the argument
4. State the conventional wisdom, then destroy it
5. Chronological build
6. Organized around three questions
7. One continuous argument, no subheads
8. Scouting-report format
9. Magazine-style feature
10. Myth-vs-reality frame
11. The debate piece (steelman both sides, then rule)
12. The receipt piece (a logged prediction and the exact conditions that grade it)

# PART 3 — ELIMINATE AI REPETITION

## Banned as house boilerplate
Each may appear at most once per article, and the wording must vary article to article. If several recent articles share a rhetorical structure, deliberately write the next one differently.

- "The honest complication is…" / "The honest counterpoint is…" / "The counterpoint is…" (max ONE counterpoint framing per article)
- "The failure condition is…" / "The condition is…"
- "The consequence is simple." / "That matters because…"
- "The question is…" / "The real question…" / "The answer…"
- "The ceiling…" / "The floor…" / "The margin…" as recurring section anchors
- "Watch ___ for the answer."
- "This isn't X. It's Y." / "The headline is X. The story is Y."
- "That is the ceiling, floor, and most likely outcome in one sentence."
- "Credit belongs to X… Credit also belongs to Y…"
- "Quickly, the supporting pieces are clear."
- "The task comes before the quarterback" / "the matchup before the matchup" constructions

The analytical *move* (steelman, name the failure state) stays. The *label announcing the move* goes. Instead of "The counterpoint is the 10-3 record," write: "Oklahoma did just win ten games, and that's not nothing — but the rushing average tells you which part of those ten wins is least likely to repeat."

## Every paragraph must earn its place
Every paragraph must do at least ONE of: reveal new information · provide evidence · advance the argument · complicate the argument · introduce a counterargument · explain why something matters · provide historical context · create personality · produce a memorable observation · move the story forward.

If a paragraph merely restates the thesis in different words, delete it. Do not say the same idea in the opening, then under a subhead, then in a quote, then in the paragraph after the quote, then in the conclusion (the Vanderbilt piece repeats its "Curtis provides a quarterback path" concept in several formulations; the Oklahoma piece cites 118.5 rushing yards three-plus times; the Castellanos piece repeats the Sorsby/ACL facts in five of seven sections). **Rule: every key fact appears exactly once in body prose** (plus the Facts box on Wire pieces). The quote-then-paraphrase-of-the-quote pattern is banned — a paragraph following a quote must extend it (context, counter, consequence), never restate it. Trust the reader. This alone makes articles feel 20–30% sharper.

# PART 4 — FOUR EDITORIAL PRODUCTS (DO NOT MAKE THEM SOUND IDENTICAL)

## TYPE 1 — THE WIRE (breaking news)
**Purpose:** tell the reader what just happened and why it matters faster and more intelligently than a generic rewrite.

**Length scales with importance:** routine development 350–550 words · significant development 600–900 · major national story 800–1,100.

Every meaningful Wire story answers: (1) What happened? (2) Why does it matter? (3) What are most people missing? (4) What changes because of this? (5) What should fans watch next? **But do not visually force five/seven sections into every 400-word story — the template scales with the news.** The older mechanical short version (e.g., the K-State injury piece: What Happened / Why It Matters / Read, restating the same left-tackle point) is retired.

- The first 100 words must communicate the news. Never bury the event beneath analysis.
- Always attribute the original reporting clearly. Never invent information that isn't in the source. Never turn uncertainty into fact.
- **"What Most People Are Missing" stays as a Pate State signature — but only when there is a genuinely non-obvious insight.** Ask: what second-order consequence does this create — elsewhere on the roster, the schedule, the playoff race, the recruiting trail, the coaching situation? If no legitimate overlooked angle exists, OMIT the section rather than manufacture one. It can never restate the headline.
- **Remove internal editorial instruction from published copy.** Lines like "The Wire's signature question: what's the story under the story?" and especially "The house analysis — identical treatment on every team's story" are template scaffolding leaking to readers. Readers want consistency of *standards*, not identical *writing*. Strip all such language from templates.

**Thin-source triage (fixes the worst failure mode):**
- Full multi-section treatment only when the story has ≥5 independent substantive facts. Castellanos qualifies; the Mt. Carmel piece does not.
- Thin source → 80–120 word brief: headline, what happened, why it matters, source link. No stat cards, no Thesis, no Watch List.
- **Never publish "the report doesn't establish / doesn't provide / isn't supplied."** The Mt. Carmel piece narrates its own missing information three times and its Watch List asks what positions the recruits play — publicly available on any recruiting service. If missing info is publicly findable, the desk looks it up and includes it. If unfindable, the piece doesn't dwell on the absence.
- Watch List items must be genuine unknowns, never facts resolvable in 60 seconds.
- **Scope filter:** the Wire's promise is breaking college football news. High school recaps belong only with real recruiting substance (positions, ratings, performance detail tied to a committed class). "Impact · low" stories don't occupy the homepage Latest module's top five.

## TYPE 2 — YOUTUBE-DERIVED NOTEBOOK ARTICLES
**Do not summarize the video. Find the best argument inside it.** Ask: *if a viewer could remember only one idea from this segment tomorrow morning, what should it be? What would someone text a friend?* Build the article around that. (The SEC-bias/JP Poll article already does this well.)

Typical length: 650–1,000 words. The transcript is the primary intellectual source. Then answer the reader's real question — **"why should I read this if I already watched Josh?"** — by enriching with 3–6 carefully chosen, verified pieces of supporting evidence (never a data dump), drawn from: returning production · SP+/FPI/model data · opponent strengths · roster turnover · snap counts · line starts · recruiting composition · transfer production · injury context · betting markets · historical comps · previous seasons under that coach · schedule inflection points · one statistic that strengthens Josh's argument · one that challenges it · implications beyond that one team.

**The equation: JOSH'S THESIS + EVIDENCE + CONTEXT + CONSEQUENCE = PATE STATE ARTICLE.** Josh provides the thesis; the article proves, tests, and expands it. Every Notebook piece must contain at least three verifiable specifics beyond the episode transcript.

## TYPE 3 — ORIGINAL LONGFORM / DEFINITIVE ARTICLES
The best written material on the site and the biggest opportunity — these must feel materially different from YouTube conversions: reported, researched, definitive. 1,200–2,500 words when warranted; never stretched to hit a count — depth comes from additional thinking and evidence, not repetition.

Example subjects: Why College Football's Superteam Era May Be Over · The 10 Games That Will Decide the Playoff · The State of Alabama Football Under DeBoer · How the Portal Changed What 8–4 Means · The Anatomy of a National Champion · Which Programs Are Built to Win the Next Five Years · the definitive preseason team guides.

Elements to draw on: strong central thesis · historical context · original research · multiple statistical lenses · Josh's analysis · counterarguments · schedule implications · roster construction · recruiting and portal movement · coaching · playoff implications · charts/graphics · historical team comps · "what everyone is getting wrong" · a strong final synthesis. A reader should bookmark or share these as the definitive resource. These also carry the greatest long-term SEO/GEO value.

## TYPE 4 — JOSH / PATE STATE COLUMNS
A smaller number of deliberately conversational, opinionated pieces so the site doesn't read like an institutional explainer. 500–900 words · minimal subheads · strong opening · strong opinion · personal language · authentic Josh-isms · one memorable ending. These should feel like sitting on the porch talking ball, not reading a research memo. (Byline per Rule 1.)

# PART 5 — CRAFT STANDARDS

## Headlines
Retire the default "TEAM: X sets the ceiling, Y sets the floor, and Z decides the season" construction (currently the majority; cap the long colon/comma form at ~20% of pieces). Rotate headline families:
- **Argument:** Notre Dame Is Good Enough to Win It All. One Position Could Stop It.
- **Counterintuitive:** The Most Dangerous Thing About Texas Isn't Arch Manning
- **Numbers/Evidence:** 8 Ranked Opponents, 5 Road Trips and One Brutal Texas Schedule
- **Josh voice:** Pate: Stop Calling the JP Poll SEC-Biased. Test It.
- **Prediction:** Why Pate Has Ole Miss at 9–3 — and What Would Make Him Wrong
- **Breaking-analysis:** Oklahoma Just Bet Big on Brent Venables. Now Comes the Hard Part.
- **Curiosity:** Everybody Is Watching Alabama's Quarterbacks. Pate Is Watching Something Else.

Headlines are specific enough to promise value without explaining the entire article. Curiosity, not deception; no clickbait. Display headline ~70 characters; longer SEO title allowed in metadata. **The dek must add information the headline doesn't contain** — a number, a stake, a tension — never restate it.

## Openings
The first 2–4 sentences are critical. No throat-clearing; create tension immediately. Start with one of: the strongest fact · the strongest opinion · an unexpected number · a contradiction · a scene · the conventional wisdom about to be challenged · a high-stakes question · Josh's most interesting observation.

Examples of the register:
- "Everybody is staring at Alabama's quarterback competition. Josh Pate thinks the season may already be getting decided five yards in front of it."
- "Texas can be one of the five best teams in America and lose three games. That's not hedging. It's what happens when eight of your opponents have legitimate playoff expectations."

## Endings
Never default to "Watch X for the answer," and never end on a product plug ("Check the Oklahoma team page…") — CTAs live in page chrome, not the final sentence. Rotate ending styles: **Prediction** (state exactly what will happen) · **Consequence** (what this means for the playoff/conference/sport) · **Callback** (return to the opening image) · **Question** (leave the unresolved tension) · **Receipt** (Josh's prediction clearly on the record) · **One-line thesis** (a sentence that crystallizes the argument). The final sentence must feel intentional.

## Memorable lines
Every major article should contain 2–4 lines a reader wants to send to somebody. The models to beat, both already on the site: *"Complaining about the model without betting against the supposed mistake is fan reaction wearing a lab coat"* and *"a chain of unlikely events wearing shoulder pads."* That register — football-smart, folksy, concrete — is the Pate State voice. Do not manufacture "writerly" sentences for their own sake; no purple prose, no forced jokes. Two or three real ones per substantial story transforms the reading experience.

## Counterarguments
A+ analysis occasionally challenges Josh's thesis rather than decorating it. Where appropriate, include "The Case Against This" or weave the opposing evidence in naturally: if Josh says Team X can win the SEC, present the strongest evidence it can't — then explain why Josh still lands where he does. Intellectually curious, not promotional. This builds trust.

## The "So what?" rule
After every major fact, internally ask *so what?* Returns 17 starters — so what does that allow them to do? Completed 68% — against what competition, under what pressure, and what does it mean for this year's projection? Coach signs a six-year extension — why does the timing matter, what expectations change? Data never sits in an article merely because it is available.

# PART 6 — FACTUAL INTEGRITY & QUOTE HYGIENE

**Never invent:** players · positions · statistics · roster status · quotes · injuries · rankings · schedule information · transfers · recruiting status. Every external factual assertion must be traceable to an approved source. One roster hallucination destroys trust faster than mediocre prose ever will.

**Verify proper nouns with special care against official rosters/school sites.** Live errors found in one day's articles: "Keelin Russell" (article) vs "Keelon Russell" (Shorts rail) — one is wrong sitewide; "Cooper Barcate" (Mensah article) looks like a transcribed, unchecked name.

**Quote hygiene:** quotes are currently raw ASR output published verbatim — "I'm 0 and2 and you're 2 and 0"; leading whitespace inside quotes. Clean to standard journalistic practice (remove ums/false starts, fix number garble, preserve meaning exactly; never alter substance). One pull quote per article maximum; additional quotes run inline with timestamp links. Adopt a house style entry for odds ("+10000," not "plus 10,000"), scores, rankings, and stat formatting.

# PART 7 — SITE-SPECIFIC BUGS TO FIX (silent trust killers, found in one day's output)

1. Castellanos piece: team chip/kicker read **"Florida State · ACC"** on a Texas Tech (Big 12) story. Transfer stories tag the destination team or both — never only the outgoing school.
2. Castellanos piece: body credits **Stewart Mandel of The Athletic**; the sourcing footer links **Yahoo Sports**. On a receipts-branded site, in-prose attribution and the footer source must match. If an aggregator surfaced the report, cite the originating outlet, "via" the aggregator.
3. Homepage teaser "Title **Bid**" vs article "Title **Chase**" — teasers must be generated from the article headline, never written separately.
4. One article carries two series tags ("The Notebook" in the hero, "The Sit-Down" in the byline). One article, one series tag.
5. Meta descriptions truncate mid-word ("give Will Hammo"). Enforce ≤155 characters with whole-word truncation.
6. Internal template language published to readers ("identical treatment on every team's story" — see Part 4, Type 1). Strip from all templates.

**These are pipeline issues, not prompt issues — add an automated pre-publish validation pass:** team/conference tag matches subject · footer source matches in-prose attribution · teaser matches headline · exactly one series tag · meta description complete · no internal-instruction strings in rendered copy.

# PART 8 — PRE-PUBLISH QUALITY GATE (run on every article)

## Checklist
1. ☐ Correct byline system (Rule 1); zero unattributed first-person under a Staff byline
2. ☐ Architecture differs from the last several published pieces (Rule 2)
3. ☐ Zero banned boilerplate (Part 3); max one counterpoint framing
4. ☐ No fact stated more than once in body prose; every paragraph earns its place
5. ☐ Every proper noun verified; quotes cleaned; one pull quote max
6. ☐ Type-appropriate length and evidence (3–6 enrichment points for Notebook; ≥5 substantive facts for full Wire; thin source → brief)
7. ☐ Metadata validation passed (Part 7)
8. ☐ Headline family varies from the last 3 published; dek adds information
9. ☐ Opening creates tension in 2–4 sentences; ending style rotated, lands on the idea

## Score the draft 1–10 on ten categories
**VOICE** — could this appear on any generic sports site? · **ORIGINALITY** — a thought the source didn't hand us? · **SPECIFICITY** — could the team names be swapped and the article still work? · **EVIDENCE** — are major claims supported? · **PACING** — redundant paragraphs? · **PERSONALITY** — at least one memorable idea or line? · **STRUCTURAL VARIETY** — does this resemble the previous five articles? · **VALUE ADDED** — would someone who watched the video still learn something? · **HEADLINE** — would a serious CFB fan click? · **ACCURACY** — every player, stat, quote, and claim checked?

**Any important story scoring below 8 in two or more categories gets rewritten before publication.**
