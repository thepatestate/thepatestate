## 1. THE MISSION

Most recruiting coverage answers:

Who committed?

The Pate State should go further.

Recruiting Intelligence should answer:

WHY THIS PLAYER?

WHY THIS POSITION?

WHY THIS PROGRAM?

WHY NOW?

HOW DOES HE FIT?

WHAT IS THIS STAFF ACTUALLY BUILDING?

The reader should understand recruiting as football roster construction, not merely the accumulation of stars.

## 2. THE CORE PROMISE

A reader should finish a Recruiting Intelligence article thinking:

“I knew they landed the player. Now I understand why the player matters.”

Or:

“I knew Georgia had the No. 3 class. Now I understand what kind of roster Georgia is building.”

Or:

“I knew this player entered the portal. Now I understand why this specific program needed him.”

The goal is to turn recruiting information into football understanding.

## 3. RECRUITING INTELLIGENCE IS NOT A STAR-RATING SERVICE

Do not simply rewrite:

- recruiting ranking
- star rating
- offer list
- commitment date
- height
- weight
- hometown
- national ranking

Those facts can matter.

They are not the article.

The Pate State should explain what the recruiting services often cannot:

How does this player fit into the football program being built?

## 4. WHEN TO USE THIS MD

Use Recruiting Intelligence when the article centers on:

CLASS CONSTRUCTION

What is a program building across an entire recruiting class?

POSITIONAL STRATEGY

Why is a staff recruiting three corners, two tackles or multiple tight ends?

PROSPECT FIT

Why does a player make football sense for this particular program?

RECRUITING BATTLE

Why are multiple major programs prioritizing the same player?

ROSTER SUCCESSION

Who is being recruited to replace players likely to leave?

TRANSFER PORTAL STRATEGY

What roster problem is a portal addition actually solving?

MULTI-YEAR ROSTER BUILDING

How recruiting decisions across several cycles are shaping the program.

DEVELOPMENT BET

Why a lower-ranked prospect may fit what a staff develops particularly well.

CLASS COMPARISON

What separates two elite classes beyond their national rankings?

## 5. WHEN NOT TO USE THIS MD

Use The Wire for straightforward news such as:

- Player commits.
- Player flips.
- Player decommits.
- Player enters portal.
- Player announces top five.
- Player schedules visit.
- Program offers prospect.
- Transfer chooses school.

Those developments may later justify a Recruiting Intelligence article.

The news and the analysis are different products.

## 6. LENGTH

Normal target:

800–1,300 WORDS

Focused prospect-fit article:

700–950 words

Class / positional strategy analysis:

900–1,300 words

Major class-building or portal strategy deep dive:

1,100–1,500 words

Multi-cycle roster analysis:

up to 1,700 words when justified.

Do not stretch a simple commitment into 1,200 words.

Depth must come from roster understanding.

## 7. THE CENTRAL QUESTION

Before writing, identify the article’s central recruiting question.

Examples:

Why does Georgia keep taking bigger defensive backs?

Why is Ohio State recruiting two elite running backs in the same class?

Is Texas fixing its offensive-line future before it becomes a problem?

Why did Alabama prioritize this three-star prospect despite offers to higher-ranked players?

What does Miami’s portal class tell us about where the staff believes its roster is still weak?

That question should organize the article.

## 8. THE MOST IMPORTANT PRINCIPLE

RECRUIT THE ROSTER, NOT THE RANKING

The Pate State should continually evaluate recruiting within the context of the actual roster.

Ask:

- Who is leaving?
- Who is aging out?
- Who has not developed?
- Who is already established?
- Which position is thin?
- Which position is overcrowded?
- Which player provides a skill the room lacks?
- Which recruit could solve a future problem?
- Which portal addition changes the timeline?

A No. 87 national prospect may be more valuable to one program than a No. 32 prospect would be.

Explain why.

## 9. START WITH THE FOOTBALL PROBLEM

When possible, identify the roster problem before describing the recruit.

Example:

WEAKER:

Georgia landed four-star offensive tackle X on Saturday.

STRONGER ANALYTICAL PREMISE:

Georgia may lose both starting tackles after the season. That makes its latest recruiting win less about adding another four-star prospect and more about protecting the 2028 offensive line before it becomes a problem.

The player matters because of the roster.

## 10. RESEARCH THE CURRENT ROOM

Before evaluating a recruit or transfer, understand the current position room.

Research where relevant:

- starter
- backup
- eligibility
- class year
- snaps
- recruiting pedigree
- previous transfers
- likely departures
- NFL possibilities
- injuries
- younger developmental players
- incoming recruits

Do not say:

He fills a major need.

until you can explain the actual need.

## 11. UNDERSTAND THE TIMELINE

Recruiting is about time.

Ask:

YEAR 1

Does he play immediately?

YEAR 2

Does a starting job open?

YEAR 3

Is this when the staff expects him to peak?

For portal players:

the timeline may be:

right now.

For high-school recruits:

the real value may not arrive for two years.

Make the timeline clear when it matters.

## 12. STARS ARE CONTEXT, NOT ANALYSIS

Recruiting rankings matter.

Use them accurately.

But do not treat:

No. 64 nationally

as the reason a player is good.

Explain:

- movement
- frame
- position
- physical traits
- production
- versatility
- skill set
- developmental fit
- competition level

when verified information supports it.

The ranking tells us how the recruiting industry views the player.

The article should explain why the program views him as valuable.

## 13. DO NOT PRETEND TO SCOUT WHAT YOU HAVE NOT SEEN

This is critical.

Do not write:

He has elite hip fluidity and advanced route recognition.

simply because a recruiting profile contains positive language.

If Pate State has not independently evaluated film:

attribute scouting assessments.

Use language such as:

247Sports describes him as…

On3’s scouting report highlights…

His verified track times suggest…

His high school production shows…

Do not manufacture film evaluation.

## 14. IF FILM HAS BEEN REVIEWED

When actual film evaluation is available:

be specific.

Explain:

- what trait shows up
- in what situation
- how it translates
- where development is needed

Avoid generic scouting adjectives:

- explosive
- twitchy
- physical
- versatile
- athletic
- instinctive

unless the article demonstrates what the word means.

## 15. THE FIT MATTERS MORE THAN THE LABEL

Ask:

What would this player actually be asked to do?

Examples:

A 225-pound edge may fit one defense as:

- stand-up rusher

and another as:

- developmental linebacker

A tight end may be recruited primarily as:

- attached blocker
- detached receiving threat
- H-back
- hybrid

A corner may project to:

- boundary
- field
- nickel
- safety

Do not assume position labels mean identical roles across programs.

## 16. EXPLAIN WHY THE STAFF WANTS HIM

Possible reasons include:

- rare position
- size
- speed
- positional flexibility
- immediate need
- long-term succession
- scheme fit
- geographic priority
- relationship
- program familiarity
- developmental upside
- roster balance

Only use reasons supported by evidence.

Do not invent private recruiting-board logic.

Phrase inference as inference.

## 17. COACHING HISTORY IS USEFUL — NOT DETERMINISTIC

A coach’s previous recruiting or development history can reveal preferences.

Example:

The staff has repeatedly recruited longer corners since arriving.

That may be meaningful.

But do not conclude:

Therefore this recruit will become Player X.

Past patterns provide context.

They do not guarantee outcomes.

## 18. DEVELOPMENT IS PART OF RECRUITING

Ask:

What does this program historically do well with this type of player?

Examples:

- Georgia developing defensive linemen
- Ohio State developing wide receivers
- Iowa developing tight ends
- Wisconsin historically developing offensive linemen

Use current and relevant evidence.

Do not rely on outdated program stereotypes without verification.

## 19. RECRUITING IS ALSO SUCCESSION PLANNING

One of the most valuable Pate State angles should be:

Who is this player eventually replacing?

Not necessarily directly.

But understand the succession chain.

Example:

2026 starter likely leaves.

2027 sophomore moves up.

2027 recruit becomes depth.

2028 recruit may become starter.

That reveals roster planning.

## 20. THE “WHY NOW?” QUESTION

A recruiting push becomes more meaningful when tied to timing.

Ask:

- Did the staff miss at this position last cycle?
- Are multiple starters leaving?
- Did a portal departure create urgency?
- Did a player fail to develop?
- Is the scheme changing?
- Is the staff trying to get younger?
- Is a geographic opportunity opening?

The timing often reveals the strategy.

## 21. CLASS CONSTRUCTION > CLASS RANKING

A class can rank No. 5 and still have a specific structural weakness.

A class can rank No. 12 and fit the roster extremely well.

When analyzing a class, ask:

- quarterback?
- offensive tackle?
- defensive line?
- corner?
- high-value positions?
- numbers by position?
- immediate vs developmental?
- elite top-end talent?
- depth?
- geographic strategy?
- roster succession?

Do not reduce a class to:

ranked No. 4 nationally.

## 22. LOOK AT POSITION VALUE

Not all recruiting wins carry the same roster value.

High-value positions may include:

- quarterback
- offensive tackle
- edge
- corner
- defensive line

depending on scheme and roster.

Explain why a recruiting battle at a premium position may matter more than the overall ranking suggests.

## 23. QUARTERBACK RECRUITING IS DIFFERENT

Quarterback decisions affect:

- other recruits
- receiver recruiting
- class perception
- transfer decisions
- roster retention
- future portal movement
- staff stability

When covering a quarterback:

look beyond the quarterback himself.

Ask what his commitment changes around him.

## 24. OFFENSIVE-LINE RECRUITING REQUIRES LONGER HORIZONS

Do not evaluate offensive-line recruiting solely through immediate playing time.

Consider:

- developmental years
- redshirts
- physical maturity
- multi-position ability
- tackle vs guard projection
- succession planning
- class balance

A tackle signed today may be solving a problem three seasons away.

## 25. DEFENSIVE-LINE RECRUITING IS ABOUT ROTATION

For many modern programs:

four starters are not enough.

Understand:

- snap rotation
- body types
- early-down vs passing-down roles
- edge vs interior
- injury depth
- developmental pipeline

A sixth defensive lineman may be strategically more important than the nominal backup label suggests.

## 26. RECEIVER RECRUITING IS ABOUT SKILL COMPLEMENT

Do not simply count receivers.

Ask whether the room has:

- vertical speed
- size
- slot quickness
- contested-catch ability
- route polish
- blocking
- return ability

A receiver may fill a missing skill rather than simply a roster spot.

## 27. DEFENSIVE-BACK RECRUITING REQUIRES ROLE CLARITY

Modern defensive backs may play:

- outside corner
- nickel
- safety
- hybrid
- STAR
- rover
- dime

Do not assume every listed corner is competing for the same job.

Explain role when known.

## 28. PORTAL RECRUITING IS A DIFFERENT MARKET

High-school recruiting asks:

What can this player become?

Portal recruiting more often asks:

What can this player solve immediately?

Portal analysis should evaluate:

- proven production
- competition level
- remaining eligibility
- role
- scheme
- injury history
- snaps available
- roster need
- transition risk

Do not treat portal rankings exactly like high-school rankings.

## 29. PORTAL FIT > PORTAL FAME

A recognizable transfer name may not be the best fit.

A less famous player may be more useful if he solves:

- offensive tackle
- center
- nickel
- run defense
- pass rush
- backup quarterback
- short-yardage running

Explain the actual roster problem.

## 30. THE PORTAL SHOULD NOT ERASE DEVELOPMENT

When analyzing roster construction, ask:

Is this program using the portal to supplement development or replace it?

This can reveal program philosophy.

Possible models:

DEVELOPMENT-FIRST

Recruit high school heavily, use portal selectively.

PORTAL-AGGRESSIVE

Fill immediate holes frequently.

HYBRID

Build core through recruiting, use portal at high-impact positions.

Do not praise or criticize a model automatically.

Evaluate whether it fits results and roster needs.

## 31. LOOK BACK ONE OR TWO CYCLES

Today’s recruiting story often began 18–24 months earlier.

Ask:

- Did a prior recruit transfer?
- Did the staff miss on a top target?
- Did a player develop faster than expected?
- Was a position under-recruited?
- Did an older class become NFL-heavy?

Connecting cycles creates deeper analysis.

## 32. RECRUITING FAILURES MATTER TOO

Not every article should celebrate commitments.

Analyze:

- position misses
- repeated recruiting losses
- class imbalance
- failure to land tackles
- quarterback instability
- reliance on portal corrections
- geographic erosion

Use measured language.

Do not declare recruiting disaster because one prospect chose another school.

## 33. ONE LOSS DOES NOT DEFINE A CLASS

Avoid:

This is a devastating recruiting loss.

unless the circumstances truly justify it.

Ask:

- Was the prospect uniquely important?
- Does the program have alternatives?
- Is the position already strong?
- Is the class early?
- Is the commitment likely to remain fluid?
- Is another prospect being prioritized?

Context determines significance.

## 34. COMMITMENTS ARE NOT SIGNINGS

Be precise.

A verbal commitment is not:

- binding
- signed
- guaranteed

When relevant, remember:

recruiting remains fluid until the appropriate signing/enrollment process is complete.

Do not over-dramatize every possible flip.

## 35. RECRUITING BATTLES SHOULD EXPLAIN WHY

Do not simply write:

Georgia beat Alabama, Ohio State and Texas.

Ask:

Why was this player so coveted?

Why did those programs want him?

Why did this school win?

Use verified factors where available:

- relationships
- scheme
- proximity
- playing time
- development history
- visits
- coach continuity

Do not invent NIL numbers or private family motivations.

## 36. NIL / REVENUE-SHARING CLAIMS REQUIRE CARE

Never invent:

- NIL amounts
- revenue-share promises
- inducements
- bidding-war figures
- contract terms

If credible reporting exists:

attribute it precisely.

Do not explain every recruiting decision as:

money.

Modern recruiting is financially influenced.

That does not mean every decision can be reduced to a number.

## 37. GEOGRAPHY STILL MATTERS

When supported by recruiting patterns, analyze:

- home-state dominance
- regional pipelines
- traditional recruiting territories
- new geographic expansion
- staff relationships

A class may reveal where a program believes it needs to win consistently.

## 38. STAFF CHANGES ALTER RECRUITING

Coordinator or position-coach changes can affect:

- relationships
- scheme fit
- player evaluations
- geographic pipelines
- commitment stability

When relevant, explain how staff movement changes the board.

## 39. RECRUITING IS ABOUT OPTIONS

A strong class creates future flexibility.

Examples:

- multiple tackle bodies
- two different receiver types
- several defensive backs with positional flexibility
- quarterback depth
- multiple edge profiles

Explain what options the roster gains.

Do not call everything “versatility.”

Show what the flexibility actually allows.

## 40. FIND THE HIDDEN PLAYER

In class analysis, do not only discuss the five highest-ranked recruits.

Ask:

Which player could matter more than his ranking suggests because of roster timing or fit?

This is a valuable Pate State angle.

But do not manufacture “sleeper” narratives without evidence.

## 41. FIND THE HIDDEN NEED

Likewise, ask:

Which position is more urgent than the recruiting ranking makes obvious?

Example:

A top-five class may still have only one true offensive tackle.

That can matter more than the headline ranking.

## 42. FIND THE ROSTER BOTTLENECK

A position can have many scholarship players but few plausible future starters.

Ask:

- how many bodies?
- how many playable bodies?
- how many proven?
- how many developmental?
- how many likely to leave?

Raw roster count can be misleading.

## 43. USE NUMBERS THAT REVEAL CONSTRUCTION

Useful recruiting numbers may include:

- number of commits by position
- blue-chip percentage
- top-100 players
- average rating
- portal additions
- returning snaps
- projected departures
- class balance
- multiple cycles at a position

But never let the numbers become the article.

Explain what they mean.

## 44. DON’T CREATE FAKE PRECISION

Avoid things such as:

Georgia has solved 73% of its future offensive-line need.

If the calculation does not represent something coaches actually care about:

do not invent it.

Use understandable numbers.

## 45. CLASS RANKING CONTEXT

When using national rankings:

identify:

- source
- date
- whether rankings remain fluid
- number of commitments if materially relevant

Early class rankings can be distorted by:

- class size
- timing
- uncommitted elite prospects

Do not overstate them.

## 46. CONSENSUS MATTERS MORE THAN ONE OUTLIER

If one service ranks a player dramatically differently:

do not cherry-pick whichever ranking makes the article more dramatic.

Use:

- consensus
- composite
- multiple-service context

when useful.

Then explain why the disagreement may exist if credible scouting information supports it.

## 47. DO NOT PREDICT NFL OUTCOMES CASUALLY

Avoid:

future first-rounder

NFL star

unless attribution or exceptional evidence supports it.

Prefer:

possesses traits that have made the position valuable at the next level

when relevant.

Recruiting projection already contains enormous uncertainty.

Maintain restraint.

## 48. HUMANIZE RECRUITING

Recruiting is also about:

- teenagers
- families
- coaches
- relationships
- home
- opportunity
- development

Do not reduce every prospect to:

- rating
- ROI
- roster asset
- positional value

Use human details when reported.

Do not invent personal motives.

## 49. DO NOT DUNK ON RECRUITS

Never ridicule:

- rankings
- commitment decisions
- decommitments
- development
- size
- performance

Critique:

- program strategy
- roster construction
- staff decisions

with evidence.

Young athletes should not become targets for content.

## 50. THE ARTICLE SHOULD FEEL LIKE THIS PROGRAM

Ask:

Why is this recruiting story specifically interesting at THIS school?

Georgia recruiting a defensive lineman is different from:

USC recruiting one.

Notre Dame recruiting a transfer has different institutional considerations than:

LSU recruiting one.

Texas A&M and Oregon may operate differently.

Program context matters.

## 51. SECOND-ORDER INSIGHT

Most substantial Recruiting Intelligence pieces should reveal something beneath the obvious news.

Examples:

The major commitment is actually insurance for a likely NFL departure.

The highly ranked class is unusually thin at tackle.

A three-star defensive back fits a role the roster currently lacks.

A portal addition allows a freshman to develop rather than play immediately.

Two quarterback commitments may affect receiver recruiting.

The reader should think:

“I hadn’t connected those.”

## 52. DO NOT FORCE CONTRARIANISM

Do not write:

Everyone loves this five-star commitment, but here’s why it might actually be bad.

unless evidence genuinely supports concern.

Being perceptive is valuable.

Being reflexively contrarian is not.

## 53. THE CHESSBOARD — RECRUITING VERSION

Use only when player fit genuinely benefits from schematic explanation.

Possible questions:

- Where would he align?
- What role does his body type fit?
- What matchup would the staff use him for?
- What package could use him?
- How does his skill set complement another recruit?

This is especially useful for:

- tight ends
- hybrid defenders
- quarterbacks
- defensive backs
- edge players
- versatile backs/receivers

Do not pretend a high-school player’s college role is certain.

Use projection language.

## 54. THE PATE STATE READ

Use for substantial recruiting analyses when a clear final argument is warranted.

Target:

100–175 words

It should answer:

What does this recruiting development tell us about what the program is building?

Do not summarize rankings.

Do not repeat the commitment announcement.

Example principle:

The real value of this class isn’t simply that Georgia has five top-100 defenders. It’s that the class addresses the positions most likely to lose experienced players after the season without forcing those freshmen to arrive as immediate saviors.

That is roster analysis.

## 55. DO NOT FORCE THE PATE STATE READ

Smaller prospect-fit pieces may end naturally.

A strong final paragraph can be better than a branded module.

Use the brand element when the story has earned a broader house judgment.

## 56. HEADLINES

Recruiting Intelligence headlines should lead with:

- meaning
- roster fit
- class strategy
- consequential player

not simply ranking hype.

WEAKER:

Georgia Lands Another Four-Star Defensive Lineman

That belongs in Wire.

STRONGER:

Georgia’s Latest Defensive-Line Commitment Fits a Bigger 2027 Roster Plan

WEAKER:

Ohio State Has the No. 2 Recruiting Class

STRONGER:

Ohio State’s No. 2 Class Is Being Built Around the Positions It Can Least Afford to Miss

The headline should make the analysis clear.

## 57. DECKS

Target:

25–55 words

The deck should answer:

Why is this more than another recruiting transaction?

Use:

- roster timing
- positional need
- class construction
- fit
- bigger recruiting battle

Do not simply repeat stars and rankings.

## 59. CLASS AUDIT MODE

For periodic class audits, answer:

WHAT IS ALREADY EXCELLENT?

WHAT IS STILL MISSING?

WHICH POSITION IS MOST IMPORTANT FROM HERE?

WHICH COMMITMENT IS MOST IMPORTANT RELATIVE TO NEED?

WHICH PLAYER MAY BE UNDERRATED BY THE CLASS RANKING?

WHAT WOULD CHANGE THE CLASS CEILING?

This can be one of the strongest recurring recruiting products on the site.

## 60. PORTAL AUDIT MODE

For portal windows, examine:

- losses
- additions
- remaining needs
- proven snaps lost
- proven snaps added
- immediate starters
- depth additions
- development implications

The core question:

Did the program solve the problems it entered the portal with?

Do not rank the portal class solely by stars.

## 61. ROSTER SUCCESSION MODE

This could become a distinctive Pate State product.

Choose a position and map:

CURRENT STARTERS

LIKELY DEPARTURES

NEXT WAVE

INCOMING RECRUITS

THE QUESTION

Example:

Georgia’s edge room looks deep today. The real question is what it looks like after two veterans leave for the NFL.

This turns recruiting into future-depth-chart intelligence.

## 62. “WHY THEY WANT HIM” MODE

For elite national prospects:

do not write generic profiles.

Explain:

- why the skill set is scarce
- what programs see
- why multiple schemes can use him
- why his position matters
- why the particular recruiting battle matters

The reader should understand why this recruit commands attention.

## 63. “WHY HE FITS” MODE

This article should combine:

VERIFIED PROSPECT PROFILE

with:

CURRENT ROSTER

with:

COACHING / SCHEME CONTEXT

Then answer:

Why does this pairing make football sense?

This is much more useful than:

Four reasons fans should be excited.

## 64. RECRUITING BOARD ARTICLES

If covering a board or priority list:

do not simply create:

- Prospect A
- Prospect B
- Prospect C

Add context:

- priority
- position need
- realistic competition
- fit
- timeline
- consequences

Distinguish between:

best player

and:

most important player for this class.

Those may not be the same.

## 65. DO NOT CONFUSE INTEREST WITH MUTUAL INTEREST

Be precise.

An offer does not mean:

- recruit strongly considers school
- school strongly prioritizes recruit
- visit is imminent
- commitment is likely

Separate:

- offer
- contact
- visit
- finalist
- priority
- commitment

based on available evidence.

## 66. CRYSTAL BALLS / PREDICTIONS

Treat recruiting predictions as:

projections

not facts.

Identify:

- source
- timing
- confidence

when relevant.

Do not write as though a predicted commitment has already happened.

## 67. RECRUITING TIMELINES CHANGE

Date matters.

Information can shift after:

- visit
- coaching change
- new offer
- commitment
- decommitment
- signing period
- NIL development

Ensure recruiting reporting is current.

Do not blend outdated recruiting information into present analysis without acknowledging timing.

## 68. VISITS MATTER — BUT DON’T OVERREAD THEM

A visit can signal meaningful interest.

It is not automatically:

a major momentum shift.

Look for:

- return visits
- official visits
- family involvement
- timing
- coach relationships
- repeated contact

Use evidence.

## 69. USE DIRECT RECRUIT QUOTES CAREFULLY

Recruiting interviews often contain polite language about multiple schools.

Do not transform:

“I had a great visit.”

into:

“School X has surged.”

unless broader reporting supports it.

Interpret with restraint.

## 70. SOURCING

Important recruiting claims should be sourced.

Especially:

- commitment
- decommitment
- visits
- final schools
- rankings
- offer lists
- staff priorities
- portal entry
- portal destination
- eligibility
- NIL/revenue information

Prefer original recruiting reporters when they broke the information.

## 71. INTERNAL PATE STATE LINKS

Recruiting Intelligence should connect readers to:

- team pages
- Wire commitments
- previous recruiting analysis
- roster/depth stories
- Josh show coverage
- class rankings
- transfer stories

A commitment article and a deeper class-construction article should reinforce each other.

## 72. MAKE RECRUITING FEEL LIKE FOOTBALL

This is the overarching style rule.

Avoid articles that read like:

- rankings database
- transaction ledger
- recruiting press release

Readers should be able to see:

where the player might eventually line up

and:

why the staff needed him there.

## 73. AVOID RECRUITING CLICHÉS

Strongly avoid overusing:

- building momentum
- huge recruiting win
- recruiting trail
- rich get richer
- loaded class
- stacked room
- major pickup
- recruiting heater
- statement commitment
- making waves
- pipeline continues
- recruiting battle is heating up

These can occasionally be accurate.

Usually there is a more specific way to describe what happened.

## 74. “MASSIVE GET” IS NOT ANALYSIS

Instead of:

This is a massive get for Georgia.

Explain:

Georgia now has two offensive tackles in the class at a time when both current starters could be gone before either freshman finishes his first year.

Specificity makes significance obvious.

## 75. DON’T OVERUSE “BLUE-CHIP”

If exact rankings are available:

use them.

If broader category helps:

fine.

Do not repeatedly describe every four- or five-star prospect as:

blue-chip talent.

The phrase quickly loses meaning.

## 76. READER CONNECTION

The audience includes fans who follow recruiting obsessively and fans who mostly care once Saturday arrives.

Write for both.

Do not assume everyone knows:

- every prospect
- every ranking
- every commitment date

But do not explain recruiting like it is a new concept.

Give only the context necessary to understand why the move matters.

## 77. ENGAGEMENT THROUGH DISCOVERY

Every 150–250 words, add something meaningful:

- roster insight
- prospect detail
- class context
- another player
- coach pattern
- statistic
- timeline
- scheme fit
- future implication

Do not pad recruiting stories with adjectives.

## 78. THE “COULD THIS APPLY TO ANY SCHOOL?” TEST

After drafting, replace the program name mentally.

If the article still works nearly unchanged:

it is too generic.

A Georgia recruiting story should feel like Georgia.

A Notre Dame recruiting story should account for Notre Dame.

An Oregon recruiting story should understand Oregon’s approach.

Program specificity is mandatory.

## 79. PRE-DRAFT QUESTIONS — PROSPECT

Before writing, answer internally:

- What exactly happened?
- What is the prospect’s verified ranking/profile?
- What position does the program project him to play, if known?
- What does the current room look like?
- Who may leave before he is ready?
- What skill or body type does he add?
- Why does this staff value him?
- Which claims come from external scouting?
- What is the development timeline?
- What does this commitment change?

## 80. PRE-DRAFT QUESTIONS — CLASS

Before writing:

- Where does the class currently rank?
- How much does class size influence that ranking?
- Which positions are strongest?
- Which positions remain incomplete?
- Which commitments matter most relative to need?
- What does the class say about the program’s strategy?
- Which current roster departures is the class preparing for?
- Is there a geographic pattern?
- Is there a player-type pattern?
- What would materially change the class from here?

## 81. PRE-DRAFT QUESTIONS — PORTAL

Before writing:

- What roster problem existed before the portal opened?
- Who left?
- How much proven production/snaps left?
- Who arrived?
- Which additions are potential starters?
- Which are depth?
- What problem remains?
- Does the portal class complement the high-school class?
- Does the strategy reveal confidence or concern in younger players?
- What does the roster look like after the moves?
