Prefer players, coaches, positions, games and decisions over abstract nouns.
BAD:

**2. STOP WRITING LIKE EVERY PARAGRAPH IS A THESIS**

- “The story is…”
- “The reality is…”
- “The question is…”
- “The key is…”
- “The clean read is…”
- “The bigger point is…”
- “The part easy to miss is…”
- “What matters here is…”
- “The polling says…”
- “The roster says…”
These can occasionally work.
They become robotic when used repeatedly.
Sometimes simply tell the reader what happened and allow the meaning to emerge naturally.

**3. USE NORMAL HUMAN TRANSITIONS**
Avoid making every paragraph connect with perfect logical symmetry.
AI loves:
X is true. But Y is also true. Therefore Z.
Real sports writing has more natural movement.
Use transitions such as:
- “And that's where this gets interesting.”
- “There's one problem.”
- “That's the bet.”
- “For Georgia, that's nothing new.”
- “Now comes the harder part.”
- “Here's where Williams matters.”

- “We'll find out quickly.”
Use these selectively.

- what an opportunity means
- how long somebody has waited
- the disappointment of losing a season
- the excitement around a young player's opportunity
Do this without becoming sentimental.

Prefer:

over:

Never use corporate language to describe football.

- roster strategy
- personnel problem
- internal answer
- production profile
- expectation territory
- postseason burden
- roster construction dynamic
- continuity equation
- developmental infrastructure
- personnel solution
- position-group outcome
- competitive landscape
- program trajectory

- public confidence
- strategic implication
These aren't always grammatically wrong.

“That's the standard now.”
“That's the bet.”

“September tells you whether it's real.”

One memorable line is stronger than six attempted memorable lines.

**9. REMOVE FAKE PROFOUNDNESS**
Delete sentences that sound profound but don't actually tell the reader anything.
Examples:
“The season has to prove what the preseason can only assume.”
“Continuity only matters until continuity is tested.”
“Expectations create their own burden.”
“The answer lies somewhere between projection and production.”
These sound polished but provide almost zero information.
Replace them with a fact, football consequence or genuine observation.

**10. DO NOT FORCE A “STORY UNDER THE STORY”**
“What Most People Are Missing” must uncover an actual overlooked detail.
Do not announce:
**THE STORY UNDER THE STORY**
and then provide another generic interpretation of the headline.
Instead find something tangible.
For Georgia:
**Obvious:** Georgia is highly ranked.
**Better hidden angle:** Georgia's relative lack of portal dependence means injuries place more immediate pressure on players the staff recruited and developed internally.
Then explain that in normal language.

**11. VARY SENTENCE RHYTHM**
AI prose often uses repeated 15–25 word declarative sentences.
Mix:
A short sentence.
A normal sentence explaining the fact.
Then occasionally a longer sentence that connects several ideas.
The paragraph should sound spoken when read aloud.
If five consecutive sentences have nearly identical length and construction, rewrite them.

**12. READ EVERY SECTION OUT LOUD**
Before finalizing, ask:
**Would a knowledgeable college football person actually say this?**
If a sentence would sound strange coming out of someone's mouth on a podcast, simplify it.

Someone might say:
“Georgia is betting that most of the answers are already on the roster.”
Nobody says:
“Williams' absence adds a personnel problem to a team already being judged by what happens after December.”
Someone might say:
“Losing Williams hurts, but Georgia's bigger test still comes in December and January.”

**13. PEOPLE > CONCEPTS**
When possible, name the person.
Instead of:
“The replacement must emerge internally.”
Write:
“Somebody already in that edge room is going to get a bigger opportunity.”
Instead of:
“Established offensive production returns.”
Write:
“Gunner Stockton and Nate Frazier are back.”
Names make writing feel reported.
Abstract categories make writing feel generated.

AI loves making every section perfectly self-contained:
Fact → interpretation → counterargument → conclusion.
Do not do that every time.
Some sections can simply:
- tell a story
- make one observation
- present three useful facts
- ask a genuinely interesting question
- explain one football mechanism
Variation makes the article feel human.

Never write:

Write:
“That gives Trickett another player he can keep on the field.”

AI overuses the word “answer.”
Avoid:
- internal answer
- dependable answer
- second answer
- offensive answer
- roster answer
- another answer

- another tight end who can catch

- a second pass-rushing option
- someone who can play 30 snaps

Specific football language sounds human.

BAD:

BETTER:
“If Maryland can put two tight ends on the field without giving away run or pass, Trickett can force defenses to choose between covering Fleming with a linebacker or getting lighter against the run.”
That is football analysis.
“More flexibility” is not.

**4. DON'T OVERSTATE COACHING HISTORY**

BAD:
“Trickett's history shows Maryland will feature tight ends.”
BETTER:
“Trickett has featured the position successfully before, which gives us a reason to think he'll use it if Maryland has the personnel.”
Past tendencies support a possibility.

- “the room faces a defining test”
- “the position must prove itself”
- “the offense must cash the check”
- “the pressure now falls on”
Unless the situation genuinely warrants that level of drama.
Most preseason position battles are interesting because of football consequences, not because they are existential crises.

GOOD:

Can the second tight end block well enough to stay on the field?
Who gets the third-down snaps?
Can Maryland play two tight ends without telling the defense what's coming?

Can the room turn opportunity into sustainable production?

BAD:

“The depth chart should become clearer after several games.”
Try:
“By the time Big Ten play arrives, Maryland should know whether it has one tight end defenses fear or two they have to account for.”
Put the analysis inside the season fans actually experience.

**8. CREATE MOMENTS OF RECOGNITION**
Occasionally describe something fans instinctively recognize.
Example:
“Everyone loves two-tight-end personnel until the second tight end can't threaten anybody as a receiver. Then the defense knows exactly why he's on the field.”
A knowledgeable fan understands that immediately.
Those moments create connection because the reader thinks:
“Exactly.”
We want several of those moments throughout the site.

**9. MAKE PLAYERS FEEL LIKE PEOPLE**
Do not turn every player into:
- production
- snaps
- eligibility
- roster depth
- personnel
When the story provides the opportunity, give the reader one human detail.
Examples:
“Szymanski has spent most of his Maryland career waiting for exactly this kind of opening.”
“A freshman who expected a developmental year can suddenly find himself preparing for meaningful October snaps.”
“Pastore spent years becoming the player Kansas State finally knew it could trust.”
Do not invent emotions.
Do not write melodramatically.
Simply acknowledge that opportunity, disappointment and development happen to actual people.

**10. USE SPECIFICITY TO CREATE IMAGERY**
Football writing becomes more engaging when the reader can picture what is being described.
BAD:
“Fleming provides matchup versatility.”
BETTER:
“Put Fleming in the slot, leave a linebacker across from him and Maryland has the matchup it wants before the ball is even snapped.”
BAD:
“Kansas State may provide additional protection.”
BETTER:
“Watch for the tight end to spend a few more Saturdays helping the new left tackle before releasing into his route.”
Let the reader see the football.

**11. VARY THE TEMPERATURE OF THE ARTICLE**
Not every paragraph should sound equally intense.
Mix:
**Reporting**
Fleming caught 40 passes last season.
with:
**Observation**
Everyone else at the position combined for nine.
with:
**Football analysis**
That's why finding TE2 matters more than simply finding Fleming a few extra targets.
with:
**Personality**
Defenses can live with one problem. Two gets annoying.
This variation makes an article feel alive.

**12. USE SHORT SENTENCES AS LANDING SPOTS**
After an information-heavy paragraph, occasionally land the idea.
Example:
Fleming is established, Howard brings experience, and Szymanski has been around the offense. Maryland doesn't need all three to become stars.
**It needs two to matter.**
Or:
Kansas State can scheme help toward a new tackle. It can move the pocket. It can keep a tight end attached.
**What it can't manufacture is experience.**
These lines work because the information earns them.
Do not manufacture one after every paragraph.

**13. SURPRISE THE READER ONCE OR TWICE**
A strong article should contain at least one observation that is not where the reader expected the argument to go.
Example:
The reader expects:
Fleming needs a bigger year.
The article discovers:
Fleming may already be good enough. The more consequential player could be whoever becomes Maryland's second tight end.
That small reversal creates engagement.
Ask before finishing:
**Did we tell the reader anything genuinely unexpected?**
If not, look harder.
Do NOT manufacture a contrarian opinion simply to satisfy this rule.
The surprise must come from the facts.

**14. BUILD A LITTLE TENSION**
Sports are interesting because something remains unresolved.
Identify the unresolved question early.
For Maryland:
**Does Trickett have one useful tight end or a tight end room he can build around?**
For Georgia:
**Is Georgia's internal development good enough to match its championship expectations?**
For Kansas State:
**How quickly can a completely rebuilt offensive line learn to play together?**
Allow that question to sit underneath the article.
Don't answer it completely until The Pate State Read.
This gives the piece narrative movement.

**15. DON'T ANSWER EVERYTHING IMMEDIATELY**
AI tends to state the thesis in the deck, repeat it in the opening, repeat it in “Why It Matters,” and repeat it again in the conclusion.
Don't.
Reveal the argument progressively.
The reader should discover the story with us.
Example:
OPEN:
Fleming caught 40 of Maryland's 49 TE receptions.
↓
WHY IT MATTERS:
Maryland also lost receiver production.
↓
WHAT PEOPLE ARE MISSING:
The second tight end may matter more to Trickett's scheme than additional Fleming targets.
↓
CHESSBOARD:
Now show exactly why two legitimate tight ends create personnel problems.
↓
PATE STATE READ:
Maryland already has the star. Finding his running mate is what could change the offense.
That creates a journey.

**16. DON'T MAKE EVERY SECTION TITLE FEEL LIKE A REPORT**
The recurring Pate State branded sections should remain.
But within those sections, the prose should not sound like a briefing document.
Avoid starting every section with:
“The key factor is…”
“The primary consideration is…”
“The relevant issue is…”
Jump directly into the interesting part.

**17. WRITE ONE OR TWO LINES PEOPLE MIGHT REMEMBER**
Do not TRY to manufacture viral quotes.
But do look for the simplest memorable expression of the article's central idea.
Examples:
“Maryland has a No. 1 tight end. Now it needs a No. 2.”
“Georgia doesn't have an August problem. It has a January question.”
“Pastore wasn't one piece of Kansas State's rebuild. He was the piece they thought they didn't have to rebuild.”
These lines work because they compress a real insight.
If the line feels clever before it feels true, delete it.
**Truth first. Clever second.**

**18. USE “WE” VERY SELECTIVELY**
Occasional shared language can create intimacy.
Example:
“We'll know pretty quickly whether Howard has become that second option.”
“Watch who stays on the field with Fleming on third down.”
This makes the reader feel like we're watching the season together.
Do not constantly say:
- we think
- we believe
- we know
- we expect
Use shared language mainly when inviting the reader to watch something.

**19. GIVE THE READER SOMETHING TO WATCH ON SATURDAY**
This may be one of the most important Pate State principles.
Great analysis changes what the reader notices the next time he watches the team.
For Maryland:
“Watch who lines up next to Fleming on obvious passing downs.”
For Kansas State:
“Watch which side the protection slides toward.”
For Georgia:
“Watch which edge defender gets the first meaningful snaps after Williams.”
If reading the article changes how someone watches the next game, we have created real value.

**20. MAKE “WHAT MOST PEOPLE ARE MISSING” THE DELIGHT SECTION**
This section should often contain the article's most satisfying insight.
The reader should get there and think:
“Okay, now I'm glad I clicked.”
It should NOT feel like mandatory template copy.
If the hidden angle isn't obvious, research more.
Look for:
- a surprising numerical imbalance
- a personnel consequence
- an overlooked player
- a scheme possibility
- a schedule implication
- an old roster decision suddenly becoming important
- a coaching tendency that changes the interpretation
This section should produce **discovery**, not another summary.

**21. MAKE “THE CHESSBOARD” VISUAL IN THE READER'S HEAD**
Do not merely tell the reader what a formation accomplishes.
Let him picture the decision.
Example:
“Maryland puts Fleming in the slot with another tight end attached. Now the defense has a choice: leave a linebacker over Fleming or replace him with a defensive back and lighten the box.”
That's a football scene.
Then explain why it matters.
The reader should almost be able to see the pre-snap picture.

**22. THE PATE STATE READ SHOULD FEEL LIKE A PAYOFF**
The conclusion should not summarize the previous 800 words.
It should resolve the main question.
The reader has already seen the evidence.
Now tell him what you believe it adds up to.
Good:
“Maryland already knows Fleming can play. Whether its tight ends actually reshape the offense depends on finding somebody defenses can't ignore next to him.”
Bad:
“In conclusion, Maryland's tight end room has several opportunities and questions heading into 2026.”
The final section should feel like the writer finally puts his cards on the table.

**23. LEAVE THE READER WITH ANTICIPATION**
Whenever possible, end on something the season will answer.
Not:
“Time will tell.”
Instead:
“The first clue comes on third down: who is still standing next to Fleming when Maryland has to throw?”
Or:
“By October, we'll know whether this was a Fleming story or the beginning of a very different Maryland offense.”
Give the reader a reason to remember the article when the games begin.

**24. THE “WOULD I KEEP READING?” TEST**
After every 150–200 words, stop.
Ask:
**If I already knew the headline, would I still want to keep reading?**
If no:
Something needs to happen.
Add:
- a new fact
- a player
- a surprising number
- a sharper observation
- a football mechanism
- a human moment
- a question
- a contrast
Never solve boredom by adding adjectives.

**25. THE CONNECTION TEST**
Before publication, ask:
**Did the article teach me something?**
**Did it make me picture football?**
**Did it contain at least one observation I hadn't considered?**
**Did I feel like the writer understood the team?**
**Did the writer sound like someone I'd want to talk college football with?**
**Did anything make me smile, nod, or think “exactly”?**
**Did the article give me something specific to watch in the next game?**
**Did I enjoy reading it — not merely finish reading it?**
If the answer to the last question is no, the article needs another pass.

**THE PATE STATE VOICE IN ONE SENTENCE**
Write like:
**A smart, curious college football obsessive who did more homework than everyone else — but talks to the reader like a friend on the front porch, not a professor at a podium.**
