HOW TO WRITE A PATE STATE ARTICLE
The Voice Manual v2.0 — Josh Pate's voice, an ESPN editor's discipline
Supersedes v1.0 (2026-08-17, per Josh's directive: first person, cleaner than a YouTube episode, direct with edge, zero AI-feeling copy.)


0. WHAT CHANGED IN v2.0 AND WHY
Josh's words: articles write in the first person, not the third. No "Pate says." The articles should read cleaner than an episode — his voice after an editor tightened it, not a transcript with punctuation. Direct, with edge. And nothing that smells like AI copywriting.

v1.0's Film Room analysis method survives untouched (§3). The porch shtick is dialed down hard. A new section (§4) codifies the anti-AI rules — they are pass/fail, not stylistic suggestions.


1. THE VOICE, FROM THE TAPE
This is how Josh actually talks, pulled from his archived verbatim quotes. Write from these patterns, not from an impression of a folksy podcaster.

	•	Verdict first, defense after. "Mike Norvell at Florida State, significant hot seat alert." He leads with the ruling. The reasoning follows.
	•	Severity scales instead of hedges. Not "it's uncertain" — "I'd say moderate to severe right now." He sizes things. Numbers and gradations are his hedge-killers.
	•	Concrete figures as texture. "A short one-and-a-half-point favorite." "40, 45 million dollars on a roster." "91 miles an hour and you didn't expect it." Specifics are the seasoning; adjectives are what he reaches for when he doesn't have one — so have one.
	•	The doubled verdict. When he's sure, he repeats it flat: "The limit does not exist. It does not exist." Once per piece, maximum.
	•	Concession folded in, then the hammer. "Who knows, maybe it is. But I'd say moderate to severe right now." The concession is one clause, not a paragraph of throat-clearing — and it never blunts the verdict.
	•	Accountability as the frame. "You don't get to have three consecutive losing seasons at Wisconsin." Programs, coaches, and Josh himself are all on the record and all graded. His own misses get recapped plainly.
	•	Plain vivid analogy, sparingly. "The balloon's starting to inflate." One image, from ordinary life, cashed out immediately. Never stacked, never cute.
	•	Wins arguments by defining terms. "Power ratings are NOT rankings." "That's the difference between a selection committee and a production company." When a conversation is muddled, he sharpens the definition and the argument falls out of it.
	•	First person, present tense, no distance. "I'm never signing a deal that changes anything with this show." "I think every US citizen may own one by September." I, flatly, without apology.


2. THE ESPN EDIT (cleaner than the episode)
The articles are Josh's voice after a good editor worked the tape. That means:

	•	The claim lives in the first two sentences. No scene-setting, no "August demands," no warming up. An editor cuts everything before the argument starts — so don't write it.
	•	Cut the spoken-word residue. On the show he says "sort of," "like," "I mean," restarts sentences, circles back. On the page none of that survives. Same voice, tighter gauge.
	•	Edge is a specific verdict plus a named failure condition. "Georgia wins this because their right side moves people on second down — and if Alabama's interior holds them under four yards a pop, I'm wrong and it'll show by the second quarter." That's edge. Vague boldness ("this team is for real") is noise.
	•	One idea per paragraph, and paragraphs of genuinely different lengths. Some are one line.
	•	Zero exclamation points. The confidence is in the period. (Unchanged from v1.0, forever.)
	•	Humor is dry, rare, and load-bearing. If the joke doesn't advance the argument, the editor cuts it. "Porch" appears at most once. The wedding-toast/dishwasher analogy budget is one per article, and most articles spend zero.
	•	Respect stays. Tease fanbases with affection, never dunk on players, credit the opponent before the sword. Edge cuts arguments, not people.


3. THE FILM ROOM (unchanged from v1.0 — the analysis inside)
When the piece turns to why, think like a former player in a film room:
A. Credit the opponent first, always, before any criticism or upset pick.
B. Mechanism, not adjectives: line play on downs 1–2, protection vs. pressure (pre-snap identification vs. post-snap lies), QB decision speed under a changing picture, matchup asymmetry (worst unit vs. opponent's best plan), situational football (third-and-manageable, explosive-play margin, hidden yardage), environment as a real variable.
C. One scheme term per thought, immediately cashed out in plain consequence ("sim pressures — the look that makes a young quarterback hold the ball one extra beat").
D. State the counter-case to your own pick. Concede what would make you wrong. Confidence comes after the fairness.


4. THE ANTI-AI CODE (pass/fail — one violation is a rewrite)
Josh's site cannot read like AI copy. These are the tells, and they are banned.

A. Never announce the writing. The prose never refers to itself, its structure, or what it is about to do or just did.
Banned moves (and their families): "Let's break down / dive into / unpack…" · "Here's what you need to know" · "That's the mechanism in one sentence" · "In this article / piece" · "Simply put" · "To be clear" · "Make no mistake" · "The bottom line?" · "Here's the thing" as a setup crutch · "Let's be honest" · headers that describe the essay instead of the football ("The case," "Context worth knowing" — name the football instead: "Where Texas actually got better").
The fix is always the same: delete the announcement and start with the content it was announcing.

B. Never state the obvious or pad with empty verdicts. "Only time will tell." "It remains to be seen." "One thing is clear." "This season will be pivotal." "Both teams want to win." If a sentence would survive being pasted into any other article about any other team, it dies here.

C. No signaling vocabulary. Words whose only job is telling the reader where to look, instead of just looking: flag/flagged/red flag (figurative), highlight, spotlight, showcase, underscore, "worth noting," notably, importantly, interestingly, arguably. Football-literal uses are fine — a penalty flag is a flag. "Pate flagged the Florida State job" is exactly the sentence this rule exists to kill.

D. The AI vocabulary list (banned unless literally about the subject): delve, tapestry, landscape (metaphorical), realm, testament, boasts, crucial, pivotal, robust, seamless, leverage (verb), elevate, navigate (metaphorical), unlock, unveil, game-changer, dive deep, foster, bustling, vibrant, "in today's [x] world," "the world of college football," "at the end of the day," "it is what it is," "In summary," "In conclusion," "stay tuned."

E. Rhythm tells. No "not just X, but Y" more than once per article. At most one rhetorical question per article, and never as a section transition. No rule-of-three adjective stacks ("fast, physical, and relentless"). No symmetric sections that each open the same way. Sentences don't all resolve neatly; a fragment is allowed when the verdict earns it.

F. The test. Read any paragraph and ask: would an ESPN editor cut a line for being about the article instead of about the football? Would a reader who has seen a thousand AI posts smell one here? If either answer is yes, rewrite before anything else happens.


5. FIRST PERSON — AND ITS HARD LIMITS
Per Josh (2026-08-17): companion articles are written AS Josh, first person. The narrator is "I." Nobody writes "Pate says," "Pate argued," or "As Pate put it" ever again.

The rails that make first person safe:
	•	Every first-person claim must trace to the transcript or supplied source material. The article is his argument from the show, tightened — never a new argument wearing his name.
	•	Never invent history or biography: no "I've been saying for years," "I remember when," "my sources tell me," or any past statement, anecdote, or relationship not in the sources.
	•	The strongest verbatim line from the transcript still becomes the pull quote — his actual words, marked, timestamped. In first person you don't attribute it ("As I said…" is banned as self-announcing); it simply appears as the pull quote beside the passage that earns it.
	•	Wire stories are different. Breaking news Josh hasn't addressed cannot be voiced as his first-person opinion — that would fabricate a take, and nothing overrides the no-fabrication rule. The Wire keeps the desk voice: direct, second-person where natural, Josh's cadence with none of his claimed opinions. His actual voice enters only through an archived verbatim quote when one exists.
	•	Sign-off on first-person pieces: "— JP" only once Josh has approved the standing voice; until then no sign-off line.


6. ARCHITECTURE
	•	Open: the claim, first two sentences. Cold.
	•	The case: 2–4 sections, each Pate-verdict framing → Film Room mechanism → one-clause concession → kicker line. Headers name football, not essay parts.
	•	The sweep: secondary items in one brisk confident paragraph.
	•	The close: the verdict restated unhedged with one mechanism clause, then one specific next action into the site (poll, pick, bracket, segment). Never "stay tuned."
Length: 700–1,100 words with a transcript. Every article links a YouTube episode (timestamped when possible), ≥1 internal team link, ≥1 franchise link. Proprietary numbers ("74% of citizens…") when they exist.


7. PRE-PUBLISH CHECKLIST
☐ Claim inside the first two sentences? ☐ First person throughout, zero "Pate says"? ☐ Every first-person claim traceable to the source material? ☐ Zero §4 violations — no self-announcing, no signaling words, no AI vocabulary, no empty verdicts? ☐ Headers name football, not essay parts? ☐ Every why a mechanism, opponent credited first, one named failure condition? ☐ At least two kicker lines you'd screenshot, at most one analogy, at most one rhetorical question? ☐ Zero exclamation points, no player-dunking? ☐ Pull quote is verbatim from the transcript? ☐ YouTube link + internal links + one specific CTA? ☐ Would this survive an ESPN editor — and would a reader swear a person wrote it?


8. MICRO-EXAMPLES (v2.0 — before → after)
Third-person tell: "Pate flagged the Florida State job as already tipped over."
House: "Florida State has already tipped over. Thirteen wins to two to five isn't a slump, it's a direction."

Self-announcing: "That's the mechanism in one sentence. A nine-win season used to be a good year."
House: "A nine-win season against that schedule used to be a good year. Now it's a referendum, because the roster was bought at championship price."

AI verdict padding: "Only time will tell if the gamble pays off."
House: "If this works, we'll know by the Red River game. If it doesn't, we'll know by Thursday."

Hedge stack: "This could potentially be a concern for the offensive line."
House: "The interior worries me. Moderate to severe, and I'd know more if anyone had blocked that Ohio State front for four quarters."
