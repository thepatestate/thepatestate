## Draft B
**The Big Ten and SEC Finally Chose a Rule for NFL Returns**

*Both conferences are targeting schools, not players, after programs explored adding athletes cut from NFL training camps. The next test arrives with LSU's Sept. 5 opener against Clemson and Auburn's Oct. 24 meeting with LSU.*

Tuesday answered one question about the transfer portal and created three more. The Big Ten and SEC decided that players who have entered the professional football system will not simply return to college football without consequences for the schools that use them. The Big Ten announced a policy that can suspend a head coach for up to 50% of a season and fine an institution millions of dollars. The SEC followed with a broader policy covering basketball and other sports, while giving its commissioner full and unlimited authority to suspend schools from competition, declare players ineligible, and discipline coaches or administrators.

That is not a suggestion. It is an enforcement structure.

[EMBED:00:08:58]

My position is straightforward: if college football does not want professional athletes returning to its rosters, then it needs a rule that says so and an enforcement mechanism that can survive review. What it cannot do is tell schools to honor an unwritten agreement while the legal system keeps exposing the limits of that agreement. Competitors do not voluntarily surrender an advantage because the rest of the sport would prefer them to. Rules exist because voluntary restraint is not a governance system.

## The argument about the five-year rule

The defense of these players is not empty. The NCAA's five-for-five rule was intended to give athletes five years to play five seasons rather than force every case through the old redshirt and injury-waiver structure. The people caught between the old system and the new one can argue that they did not receive a fair opportunity to understand what their decisions would mean. A player who declared for the draft before the change knew what the old calendar looked like, not necessarily what the new one would become.

That argument has a boundary, and the boundary is professional football. These athletes were in NFL training camps or preseason games. Some tried out for teams and were released. Whatever distinction exists between playing a regular-season NFL game and attending a professional camp, the football reality is plain: these players entered a professional environment and then sought a return to college competition.

That changes the question. This is not simply an eligibility dispute about a student who needs one more season. It is a question about whether college programs can use the professional system as another roster evaluation period and then bring that experience back into a college locker room when the opportunity becomes attractive.

The honest concession is that a court may treat the cases differently from the way athletic departments treat them. That is why the next meaningful date is Aug. 27, 2026. If player challenges arrive as expected, the conferences will have to explain how their policies fit with the court rulings already referenced in this dispute. We will know more when an actual challenge tests the language instead of a television segment testing the argument.

## The conferences targeted the institutions

The most important detail in Tuesday's announcements is where the punishment lands. The enforcement threat, as I read it, is aimed at schools and the people who authorize participation. The conferences are not primarily saying that a player will be fined or suspended by the league. They are telling a school that it can play the athlete if it chooses, but the school and its leadership will absorb the consequences.

That distinction becomes real on a Saturday schedule. LSU opens against Clemson on Sept. 5, then plays Louisiana Tech on Sept. 12 before traveling to Ole Miss on Sept. 19. If LSU were to use a player who fell within the SEC's prohibition, the question would not stop at whether that player could line up. It would move to whether the institution was willing to risk a coach suspension, a major financial penalty, or a larger sanction against the program before a schedule that also includes Texas A&M, Alabama, Texas, Tennessee, and Arkansas.

The same decision would confront Auburn before its Oct. 24 home game against LSU. Auburn went 5-7 in 2025 and opens this season against Baylor on Sept. 5, with Georgia, Ole Miss, LSU, and Alabama waiting later on the schedule. A program can decide that an available player improves its roster. It also has to decide whether that improvement is worth placing the head coach and institution directly under the commissioner's enforcement authority.

That is the mechanism the conferences were missing when they issued statements about what should happen. A statement expresses a preference. A policy assigns responsibility, names penalties, and gives the decision-maker authority to act. The Big Ten and SEC chose the second option.

## The rule is the point

I understand why some people want to separate a player who played in the NFL from a player who only attended a training camp. I understand why the five-for-five transition creates sympathy for athletes whose eligibility was affected by a rule change. But if the distinction is important enough to determine who can play, it belongs in the rulebook. If it is not written clearly, every program has an incentive to test the edge of it.

That is how the sport arrived here. Each time college athletics tries to enforce a boundary, somebody finds a legal argument against the boundary. The response cannot be an appeal to better behavior. If professional experience creates an advantage that college football wants to prohibit, then college football has to prohibit it in language that can be enforced. If the sport cannot enforce that restriction, then the sport has to accept the result instead of acting surprised when a program explores it.

The SEC's decision to extend the policy beyond football makes the principle broader. This is not only about one roster, one coach, or one conference rivalry. It is about whether the major conferences believe they can establish participation standards for their members and attach consequences to violations. That makes the legal challenge more likely, not less, because the policy now reaches across multiple sports and gives the commissioner sweeping authority.

I do not know whether LSU, Auburn, or another program will decide that a professional return is worth the risk. The SEC schedule gives those schools plenty of high-leverage games in which a roster decision could carry immediate attention, but attention is not the same thing as a legal defense. The next date to watch is Aug. 27, 2026, for the first player challenge. The next football test is Sept. 5, when LSU and Auburn begin seasons under a policy that did not exist when these roster questions started.

I have logged my prediction that players will challenge the conferences on Aug. 26, 2026, to the Ledger, with the call graded on Aug. 27, 2026. If that challenge arrives, the argument moves from conference policy to enforceability. If it does not, the institutions still have to decide whether the threat alone is enough to keep these players off the field.

The answer should not depend on which school finds the better lawyer or which commissioner gives the sharper statement. Write the rule. Apply the rule. If the rule cannot survive that process, change it in public and let every program operate under the same standard.
