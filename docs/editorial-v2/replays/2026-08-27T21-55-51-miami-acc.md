**Five Popular College Football Predictions That May Age Poorly in 2026**

*The strongest preseason takes are not always the loudest ones. Oregon’s playoff reputation, Miami’s ACC standing, Texas’ title expectations, Indiana’s new standard, and Michigan’s coaching hire all come with a specific way to be tested.*

The worst predictions in college football are not always the strangest ones. Sometimes they are the ideas that make enough sense to get repeated until nobody remembers where the evidence ends and the habit begins.

That is what I was looking for in these five submissions. Not the most outrageous sentence on the internet. I wanted the popular prediction that takes one concern, stretches it across an entire season, and treats the conclusion as settled before the first Saturday arrives.

[EMBED:00:00:24]

Some of these takes are wrong. Some contain a real concern buried inside them. That distinction matters, because a playoff loss is not automatically a permanent character trait, a thin quarterback room is not automatically a conference forecast, and a coaching hire cannot be graded before the coach has run a game.

**Oregon’s playoff reputation is not a scouting report**

The prediction that Oregon always chokes in the Playoff has become one of those sentences people use as if the word always completed the analysis. It does not.

Oregon went 13-2 in 2025, finished 8-1 in Big Ten play, and enters this season No. 2 in both the AP poll and the coaches poll. The Ducks also won their first Playoff game last season and shut out Texas Tech, 23-0. They later lost to a team that went on to win the national championship. That is not the same thing as proving Oregon cannot win the next one.

I understand why the criticism keeps returning. Dan Lanning’s program has reached the stage where getting close is no longer the accomplishment. The standard is now the last game, the late possession, the protection call against a pressure look, and the defensive communication when the opponent has one more answer. Oregon has been beaten in those settings. That is evidence of what happened, not proof of what must happen forever.

The 2026 schedule gives us several chances to check the claim. Oregon visits USC on September 26, travels to Ohio State on November 7, hosts Michigan on November 14, and finishes against Washington on November 28. Those games will show whether the Ducks can carry the same level of execution through the part of the calendar where every down starts carrying more weight.

I tend to believe Oregon eventually breaks through. I also think the people waiting for the next collapse are relying on history more than football. If the protection fails against Ohio State, if the offense becomes dependent on one answer, or if the defense cannot finish a late possession, the criticism will have fresh evidence. Until then, the meme is doing work the tape has not earned.

**Miami can be the clear ACC favorite and still have one dangerous weakness**

I am one of the people who believes Miami is the best team by far in the ACC and that everyone else is playing for second. Miami went 13-3 last season, finished 6-2 in conference play, and sits at No. 7 in both preseason polls.

The schedule will not let that opinion sit untouched. Miami visits Clemson on October 3, hosts Florida State on October 17, and travels to Notre Dame on November 7. By the time the Hurricanes leave South Bend, we should have a much better idea whether the conference gap is real on Saturdays or merely comfortable on preseason boards.

The concern is the quarterback room. There is a massive drop-off between QB1 and QB2 at Miami. That is not a prediction that the starter will miss time. It is a recognition that one absence could change the entire operation. The backup would have to handle the protections, throws, checks, and situational decisions without giving opposing defenses a simpler problem to solve.

If Miami’s starter is healthy and the team looks largely like the roster people expect, I still have the Hurricanes as the clear team to beat in the ACC. If he is unavailable, the conference race changes immediately. Both statements can live in the same column.

**Indiana’s standard changed after one perfect season**

Indiana went 16-0 in 2025 and won the national championship. That season changed the scale. An 8-4 record would have been a phenomenal year for Indiana football not long ago. After a perfect championship season, it would produce a completely different conversation.

I have not called the Indiana coach the new Nick Saban if that means six more national titles are waiting to be collected. I have seen the argument that he is the No. 1 coach in the sport, and I can understand why the temperature could cool if this season becomes difficult. That is different from predicting a collapse before the games begin.

Indiana hosts Ohio State on October 17, visits Michigan on October 24, hosts USC on November 14, and travels to Washington on November 21. Those games will tell us whether the defending champion is still handling pressure with the same precision, whether the operation protects the football in high-leverage situations, and whether the crown is producing confidence or weight.

Nothing that happens next would be crazier than Indiana rising all the way to a national championship. A step back would be possible. It would not erase the evidence from last year, and it would not make the title an accident.

**Texas is carrying a title expectation that changes the meaning of failure**

Texas as a national championship favorite is not a foolish preseason position. The Longhorns went 10-3 in 2025 and enter 2026 at No. 5 in the AP poll and No. 4 in the coaches poll. The more severe prediction is that Texas will not be in the picture at all.

That would run against the way most of the sport sees this team, and the schedule provides immediate tests. Texas hosts Ohio State on September 12, visits Tennessee on September 26, travels to Oklahoma on October 10, and later goes to Missouri and LSU before finishing at Texas A&M on November 27.

If Texas goes 9-3 and misses the Playoff, the conversation around the Texas coach would be unlike anything we have seen in college football. If the Longhorns go 10-2, make the field, and lose early, the response could still be severe because the expectation is not simply to qualify. The expectation is to contend for the championship, and that puts a professional-sports level of pressure on a college program.

I am not predicting either result. I am saying the record and the postseason finish will be judged together. Texas can have a good season by an ordinary standard and still produce a failure by the standard attached to this team.

**Michigan’s hire deserves an alternative, not just a verdict**

The prediction that Michigan made a bad hire may eventually be correct. If the Wolverines finish 8-4 and the quarterback shows no improvement, the case will be much easier to make.

But before the season begins, I want the people making that prediction to answer one question: if Michigan did not hire Kyle Whittingham, who should it have hired? This was not a routine coaching search. The circumstances around the previous coach were unusual, and the instability at athletic director was already known throughout the coaching market. Those conditions do not guarantee that the hire works. They do explain why the alternative was not obvious.

Kenny Dillingham was one possible name, but there was no way to know in that moment whether Michigan was his destination or leverage for a better deal at Arizona State. He stayed in the desert and received that better deal. That is a smart move by Dillingham, but it is not evidence that Michigan had an obvious replacement sitting on the board.

Michigan still has to install the operation, develop the quarterback, and prove the staff can function under the weight of that job. If the Wolverines finish 8-4, the criticism will have a record behind it. In late August, it has a conclusion without a game.

These five predictions all confuse a concern with a sentence. Oregon has not won the title yet, but its past losses do not make winning impossible. Miami can be the ACC favorite while carrying a quarterback-depth problem that would alter the race. Indiana can regress from 16-0 without becoming ordinary. Texas can miss the Playoff and create a larger conversation than its record alone suggests. Michigan can be questioned without pretending the alternatives were obvious.
