Preseason Annual No. 2 in the JP Poll · The Definitive Citizen's Guide

Ohio State 2026: Why the Preseason No. 1 Rides on a Rebuilt Defense, a Year-Two Quarterback Leap, and One Offensive Lineman's Position Change

Everything this program did between January and August was a response to 34 days last winter — December 6 in Indianapolis, December 31 in Arlington. The full case: the autopsy, the Hartline question, the depth chart audited, all thirteen games called, and the flag plant.

Photo Slot — Lead Art · The Shoe

The rebuild in scarlet. Eleven draft picks out, fifty-one newcomers in, and one question wearing burnt orange waiting in Week 2. · Photo credit slot

Preseason team previews are the sport's most dishonest genre. Every roster is deep, every quarterback had a great summer, every coach saw "a different look in his eye." If you printed them all out and read them back in December, you'd think 40 teams went undefeated.

Then I write one anyway. And I'll tell you why this one is different: Ohio State doesn't need my imagination. Ohio State needs my honesty about 34 days last winter — because everything this program did between January and August was a response to those two nights.

Here's the payload early, because you didn't come here for a mystery novel. Ohio State is No. 2 in the JP Poll at 94.9, a tenth of a point behind Georgia. The AI Predictor disagrees with me — it has the Buckeyes No. 1 outright, and by its math it isn't particularly close. I have them 11-1, in Indianapolis, in the playoff, and carrying the highest ceiling in America. And I have exactly one reason for the hedge, which we'll get to, because it's wearing burnt orange and it's waiting in Week 2.

Part IStart With the Autopsy

Twelve and oh. A 27-9 win in Ann Arbor that ended a four-game losing streak in The Game. A defense that gave up nine-point-three points per game — the best in America — and an offensive line that allowed six sacks in twelve games.

Then Indiana beat them 13-10, and Miami beat them 24-14, and that same line allowed ten sacks in two games. Six in twelve. Ten in two. That ratio is the entire 2026 season compressed into one sentence, and I need you to sit with it before we go any further.

Credit where it's due, and I mean every word: those weren't flukes and they weren't chokes. Indiana won the national championship. Miami played them for it. Both won the exact same way — they won first and second down, made third-and-manageable disappear, and turned the most accurate quarterback in Big Ten history into a guy operating behind a moving pocket with two hundred pounds of bad news arriving early.

Look at the third-down ledger, because it's the cleanest diagnostic in football. Ohio State's defense allowed a 31 percent conversion rate last year. All season, exactly three offenses converted 40 percent or better against them: Penn State (in a 38-14 Ohio State win), Indiana, and Miami. Two of the three are the two losses. When Ohio State controlled third down, they were 12-0. When they didn't, they were 0-2.

The lazy takeaway says "Ohio State can't win the big one." Throw that out — the 2024 team is still holding a trophy that says otherwise. The honest version is narrower and scarier: this roster was never out-talented. It got out-leveraged at the line of scrimmage twice, by the only two teams physical enough to do it. Fix the leverage, you fix the season.

"Ohio State didn't lose a talent argument in December. They lost the line of scrimmage twice, and everything else was just the receipt printing."

Part IIThe Offense — Where the Case for No. 1 Lives

The Quarterback Question Isn't the One You Think

Julian Sayin completed 77 percent of his passes last season. Big Ten record. He threw for 3,610 yards, 32 touchdowns against eight picks, and finished as a Heisman finalist as a first-year starter — the only returning Maxwell finalist in the country. If you're grading accuracy, the conversation ended in November.

I'm not grading accuracy. Completion percentage measures what happens when the picture is clean. Comfort measures what happens when the picture lies. In the Cotton Bowl, Miami's front changed the math late — showed him one thing, brought another — and made him hold the ball a half-count longer than he wanted. A half-count against that front is the difference between 77 percent and two interceptions in an elimination game. He still threw for 287 that night with the walls falling in. The talent was never in question.

And there's a split inside his season that the highlight reels skip. Against the three elite defenses on the schedule — Texas in the opener, Indiana, Miami — this offense averaged 12.6 points. Against everyone else, nearly 39. The entire year-two question is whether the wall moves. Which brings us to the man now holding the call sheet.

The Hartline Question — the New Play-Caller and the Best Player Alive

Ohio State's biggest offensive question isn't whether it has enough talent. It's whether Brian Hartline's offense will make defenses pay for covering Jeremiah Smith. One criticism that followed Hartline's NFL stint was that his offenses didn't lean on their stars enough or fix the structure around them. A coordinator can have the best receiver in America on the field and still let the scheme decide where the ball goes.

The counter-case is sitting in Ohio State's own numbers, and I'll state it like a fair witness: a 77 percent completion rate and a 32-to-8 ratio don't describe an offense that mismanaged its stars, and a building willing to play Bo Jackson, Isaiah West, and Chris Henry Jr. young has no excuse not to feature its best player. Ryan Day is the guardrail here; I've learned not to question him. This isn't a prediction that the structure fails. It's a named failure condition: if Sayin hits the elite-defense wall again because the offense won't consistently put Smith in the best answers, Hartline owns the problem.

Watch Smith's targets against Texas in Week 2. If the ball finds him early and often, this worry fades fast. If it doesn't, the talent won't be the issue. The scheme will.

What Jeremiah Smith Actually Does to a Defense

"Best player in college football" is an adjective, and adjectives don't win games, so here's what he mechanically does: Smith forces a safety to declare early. When the free safety has to shade his way before the snap — and against Smith he does, every snap — the middle opens for the tight end, the run box loses half a defender, and every other receiver is functionally single-covered. He doesn't just win his matchup. He rigs everyone else's.

And the wrinkle nobody had to deal with last year: Chris Henry Jr. — the five-star freshman built like he was designed in a lab that only had film of Smith — has been the loudest name in camp. If Henry is even 70 percent of what the recruiting industry thinks, the safety's pre-snap decision goes from uncomfortable to unfair. Pick a side of the field to be wrong on. One man can't beat a defense. He can make eleven men defend nine.

💪 Strengths
  • QB-WR tandem — a returning Heisman finalist throwing to the consensus best player in America.
  • Line continuity — nearly everyone back from a six-sacks-in-twelve-games unit. The most predictive preseason stat nobody graphs.
  • The developmental machine — reloaded a top-five defense in back-to-back years. Larry Johnson still coaches the line, which is like finding out gravity still works.
  • Motivation with receipts — every leader watched Indiana celebrate twice.
⚠️ Weaknesses
  • Eight new defensive starters — chemistry has a calendar, and the schedule doesn't wait for it.
  • Proven pass rush — the portal answers are talented; "proven against Big Ten tackles in November" is a different word.
  • Depth behind the stars — the two-deep is fine. The three-deep is a prayer.
  • The kicking margin — the season died 13-10. The operation cost real points at real moments, and nobody in Columbus wants to talk about it. I just did.

Part IIIThe Defense — Nine-Point-Three Is Gone. Now What.

Fair witness time: the best scoring defense in America got gutted. Downs, gone. Both All-American linebackers — Styles and Reese — gone. Igbinosun, gone. Eleven Buckeyes heard their names in the draft. Credit the pessimists first: chemistry in a secondary is a language, and this one has as many as eight players learning it simultaneously.

But the reload was surgical, not desperate. Up front: the Alabama package deal — interior wrecker James Smith and edge Qua Russaw — plus UCF nose tackle John Walker to eat the A-gaps, joining Kenyatta Jackson and the breakout candidate Zion Grady. At linebacker: Payton Pierce, the year-two leap of Riley Pettijohn, and Wisconsin's Christian Alliegro — a man who broke his arm against Ohio State last season and stayed in the game. In the secondary: Devin Sanchez is the next great Ohio State corner, Jermaine Mathews Jr. finally gets his boundary job with Georgia transfer Dominick Kelly pushing him, and Earl Little Jr. arrives from Florida State as the All-ACC adult in a young room.

Matt Patricia's first-year answer to losing eight starters was the No. 1 scoring defense in the country. Now he runs it back with a room he picked himself. The question was never whether this defense will be good. The question is whether it's good by October 17, because the schedule doesn't grade on a curve.

Part IVThe Opponent's View — What the Other Sideline Sees

Against the offense: bracket Smith late and lie to the kid. Rotate the bracket after the snap, not before — make Sayin's pre-snap read correct and his post-snap read wrong. Sim pressure on the downs where the protection has to declare: show six, bring three, let the half-count do the damage. And attack the left tackle with games and twists, because whoever plays there is either a sophomore on the blind side or a guard out of position. The blueprint doesn't try to beat Jeremiah Smith. It tries to make throwing to him expensive and throwing away from him uncomfortable — and it worked twice.

Against the defense: sprint before the language exists. Tempo, motion, bunch, and a deliberate hunt for the nickel — the openest job on the unit. The Texas game arrives in Week 2 for a reason every opposing staff has noticed: it's the one date where the blueprint meets the defense before the defense has met itself.

And the counter, because blueprints age: brackets get punished the day Chris Henry proves he's real, sim pressures die when a quarterback's second year syncs his clock, and tempo stops working when communication calcifies — usually around week six. The season question is whether the expiration dates arrive before October 17 or after it. The schedule gives Ohio State exactly five games to get there.

Part VThe Pate State Awards

🔗
Linchpin of the Season

Austin Siereveld, OL

Not Sayin. Not Smith. A blocker — because his success determines two positions. If sophomore Ian Moore holds at left tackle, Siereveld kicks inside to right guard and Ohio State gets a top-three line reinforcing the exact spot Indiana and Miami attacked. If Moore doesn't hold, Siereveld bounces back outside and the whole December problem re-opens. One body, two positions, the entire ceiling.

📈
Breakout Player

Zion Grady, EDGE

The whisper name of the summer. He'll rush all season next to James Smith, and interior linemen who command doubles are how edge rushers get one-on-ones. Grady won't create most of his opportunities. He just has to finish them.

🚀
Most Improved

Julian Sayin, QB

Yes, the Heisman finalist. The largest jump available on this roster is the gap between "77 percent when the picture is clean" and "unbothered when the picture lies" — worth more points than any position battle on the team.

🌟
Freshman to Watch

Chris Henry Jr., WR

The sport's cruelest coverage question: bracket Smith and live with Henry one-on-one, or play it straight and watch Smith end you. Five-star freshmen usually need runway. This one needs a defense to be wrong once.

🎯
Most Important Newcomer

Earl Little Jr., S

Eight new starters need one voice that's played winning football at an All-ACC level. Communication in the back end is the difference between a busted seam in Bloomington and a punt.

Part VIICeiling, Floor, and the Honest Middle

The Ceiling
13-0 · Wire-to-wire · The whole thing

Ian Moore is simply good, immediately. The defense's communication calcifies by week four instead of week six, the Austin night becomes the launch instead of the hedge, Henry makes brackets illegal — and the Predictor gloats at my expense all winter. Probability: real, and higher than any other roster's ceiling case in America.

The Floor
9-3 · A bracket sweat

Moore doesn't hold, Siereveld bounces back outside, and the December protection leak re-opens just as the schedule serves Texas, a Kinnick night, and Bloomington inside six weeks. Tempo teams feast until November, the kicking operation costs one more three-point game. Nine wins against this schedule still probably makes the field — and would still feel like a funeral on High Street.

The Honest Middle — The Flag Plant
11-1 · Indianapolis

One road loss before the defense exists, then the most talented roster in America becoming itself on schedule: blind side by September, communication by mid-October, kicking by the first three-point November night.

Part VIIIThe Things Nobody Puts in the Preview

The special teams reckoning. The season ended 13-10. In a three-point loss, every missed kick from the previous three months stops being a footnote and becomes a co-author. I'm not naming names and I'm not piling on a kid — that's not what we do here. I'm telling you the position group, as a group, has to be better, and nobody in a preview magazine will spend a paragraph on it. I just did.

The bye-week paradox. Teams with a first-round playoff bye are 0-5 in quarterfinals. The thing every team plays all season to earn might be a competitive disadvantage until somebody solves the rust problem. Watch how Ryan Day talks about December this year.

The market is arguing with itself, and both sides are right. A 9.5 win total on a +600 title favorite isn't a contradiction — the market prices the itinerary in the first number and the roster in the second. Ohio State is simultaneously the hardest good team to trust for twelve Saturdays and the easiest one to pick for the last four. Every argument you'll have about this team all season is those two numbers arguing with each other.

The depth audit is coming in November. Thirty portal departures mostly came from the back of the roster, and in September nobody notices the back of a roster. In November, everybody does. Championships are won by the 44th-best player on the roster in a game you barely remember.

The Michigan subplot with no precedent. Kyle Whittingham's first Game, in the Shoe, against a program that hasn't celebrated this rivalry at home in eight years. There is no film on what this matchup looks like now. That should concern and comfort both fan bases in equal measure.

🤖 The Machine's View — Where the Predictor and I Split

The AI Predictor has Ohio State No. 1 — and simulating the season ten thousand times, it handed the Buckeyes the title more often than anyone. Its case: the nation's best returning QB efficiency profile, line continuity, and a talent composite nobody matches. What it can't weigh is my tenth-of-a-point hedge: eight strangers learning one language on defense, with the final exam scheduled before the first study session.

And note the inversion running through this series: the machine ranks Ohio State first and crowns them; it ranks Notre Dame third and doesn't. I rank Ohio State second — and my champion lives in South Bend. Man and machine, each betting against his own board in opposite directions. The Model page keeps the season-long score, and one of us buys the other's argument in January.

Part IXThe Flag Plant

Here's the record, on the record: 11-1. The loss is September 12 in Austin — the one night all year where the timing genuinely favors the other sideline. Then the rebuilt defense gets five weeks to become itself, Ohio State walks into Bloomington as the better team and wins, holds serve through the gauntlet, wins The Game at home for the first time in eight years, and goes back to Indianapolis with a score to settle.

The month-by-month shape, so you can grade me in real time: September is the proof-of-life month. October is the language month — the defense either speaks fluently by Bloomington or the season becomes the floor case. November is the depth month. If the calendar goes proof, language, depth — in that order — the flag plant holds.
