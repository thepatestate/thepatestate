No. 02 · Ohio StateThe Autopsy

### Start With the Autopsy

Why 2025 ended · What the offseason answered
Preseason previews are the sport's most dishonest genre. Every roster is deep, every quarterback had a great summer, every coach saw "a different look in his eye." Print them all out and read them back in December and you'd think 40 teams went undefeated. Then I write one anyway — because Ohio State doesn't need my imagination. Ohio State needs my honesty about 34 days last winter, December 6 in Indianapolis and December 31 in Arlington, because everything this program did between January and August was a response to those two nights.
Twelve and oh. A 27-9 win in Ann Arbor that ended a four-game losing streak in The Game. The No. 1 scoring defense in America at 9.3 a game, behind a line that allowed six sacks in twelve games. Then Indiana beat them 13-10, Miami beat them 24-14, and that same line allowed ten sacks in two games. Six in twelve. Ten in two. That ratio is the entire 2026 season compressed into one sentence, and you should sit with it before going any further.
Photo slotDecember 6, IndianapolisGame photo · the 13-10 night
The night the offseason started. Indiana's front won first and second down, and everything else followed.
Credit where it's due, and I mean every word: those weren't flukes and they weren't chokes. Indiana won the national championship. Miami played them for it. Both won the exact same way — they won first and second down, made third-and-manageable disappear, and turned the most accurate quarterback in Big Ten history into a man operating behind a moving pocket with two hundred pounds of bad news arriving early.
The third-down ledger is the cleanest diagnostic in football. Ohio State allowed a 31 percent conversion rate last season. Exactly three offenses beat 40 percent — Penn State (in a 38-14 Ohio State win), Indiana, and Miami. Two of the three are the two losses. When Ohio State controlled third down, they went 12-0. When they didn't, 0-2.
"They didn't lose a talent argument in December. They lost the line of scrimmage twice — and everything else was just the receipt printing."— The thesis of this annual
The lazy takeaway says Ohio State can't win the big one; the 2024 trophy says otherwise. The honest version is narrower and scarier: this roster was never out-talented. It got out-leveraged at the point of attack, twice, by the only two teams physical enough to do it. Fix the leverage, you fix the season — and every move between January and August was aimed at exactly that.

No. 02 · Ohio StateThe Offense

### The Championship Case

Sayin · The Hartline Question · Smith's Gravity · The Domino
The accuracy is proven. What's not proven is what happens when the defense disguises the picture. Julian Sayin completed 77 percent of his passes last season — a Big Ten record — with 3,610 yards and 32 touchdowns against eight picks, a Heisman finalist as a first-year starter and the only returning Maxwell finalist in the country. If you're grading accuracy, the conversation ended in November. This annual is grading something else.
Completion percentage measures what happens when the picture is clean. Comfort measures what happens when the picture lies. In the Cotton Bowl, Miami's front changed the math late — showed him one thing, brought another — and made him hold the ball a half-count longer than he wanted. A half-count against that front is the difference between 77 percent and two interceptions in an elimination game. He still threw for 287 that night with the walls falling in. The talent survived December. The half-count didn't.
Photo slotJulian SayinGame photo · pocket, eyes downfield
The half-count question. The arm was never the issue. The disguised picture was.
And there's a split inside his season the highlight reels skip: against the three elite defenses on the schedule — Texas, Indiana, Miami — this offense averaged 12.6 points. Against everyone else, nearly 39. The entire year-two question is whether that wall moves. Which brings us to the man now holding the call sheet.
The Hartline question. Ohio State's biggest offensive question isn't talent — it's whether Brian Hartline's offense makes defenses pay for covering Jeremiah Smith. One criticism followed Hartline's NFL stint: his offenses didn't lean on their stars enough or fix the structure around them. A coordinator can have the best receiver in America on the field and still let the scheme decide where the ball goes. The fair-witness counter sits in Ohio State's own numbers — 77 percent and 32-to-8 don't describe mismanagement, and a building willing to play Bo Jackson, Isaiah West, and Chris Henry Jr. young has no excuse not to feature its best player. Ryan Day is the guardrail; I've learned not to question him. But the failure condition gets named: if Sayin hits the elite-defense wall again because the offense won't put Smith in the best answers, Hartline owns the problem.
The rest of the case is the boring stuff that wins titles: Bo Jackson back as a 1,000-yard sophomore with Isaiah West behind him, a line returning nearly everyone from the six-sack season, and a two-for-one tight end rebuild — Hunter Welcing to catch, Mason Williams to block — that tells you exactly how this staff thinks about the position.

#### The Most Important Position Battle in America

Left tackle, right guard, and the whole December question

Nobody outside of Columbus is talking about it, and it will decide more than any quarterback battle in the country. Ian Moore, a 6-6, 312-pound sophomore, is being handed the blind side. That single evaluation moves Austin Siereveld — fourteen career starts at tackle, the most versatile lineman on the roster — inside to right guard, where Indiana and Miami won the December games. No. 02 · Ohio StateThe Defense

### Nine-Point-Three Is Gone. Now What.

What left · What arrived · The clock
Fair witness first: the best scoring defense in America got gutted. Caleb Downs, gone. Both All-American linebackers — Styles and Reese — gone. Igbinosun, gone. Eleven Buckeyes heard their names called in the draft, and as many as eight of eleven defensive starters walk out the door at once. Chemistry in a secondary is a language, and this one has eight players learning it simultaneously.
But the reload was surgical, not desperate. Up front: the Alabama package deal — interior wrecker James Smith and edge Qua Russaw — plus UCF nose tackle John Walker to eat the A-gaps, joining Kenyatta Jackson and breakout candidate Zion Grady. Larry Johnson still coaches the line, which is like finding out gravity still works. At linebacker: Wisconsin's Christian Alliegro — who broke his arm against Ohio State last season and stayed in the game — anchors a room with Payton Pierce and the year-two leap of Riley Pettijohn. In the secondary: Devin Sanchez is the next great Ohio State corner, Jermaine Mathews Jr. finally gets his boundary job with Georgia transfer Dominick Kelly pushing him, and Earl Little Jr. arrives from Florida State as the All-ACC adult in a young safety room.
Pate State original artThe Shoe, LitSaturday night in Columbus
The reload was surgical. An SEC-proven pair inside, a space-eater at the nose, the same man coaching the room — and a city that expects the new eleven to speak one language by mid-October.
Matt Patricia's first-year answer to losing eight starters was the No. 1 scoring defense in the country. Now he runs it back with a room he picked himself. The question was never whether this defense will be good. The question is whether it's good by October 17 — because tempo teams will sprint before the language exists, hunt the open nickel job, and test whether eight strangers can finish each other's sentences.
The margins, while we're being honest: the two-deep is fine; the three-deep is a prayer, and thirty portal departures came out of the back of the roster — the part nobody notices until November audits it. And the kicking operation is on notice. The season ended 13-10. In a three-point loss, every missed kick from the previous three months becomes a co-author. The group, as a group, has to be better, and nobody else will spend a paragraph on it. This annual just did.

#### Jeremiah Smith

Pate State Rank: No. 1 Player in America · No. 1 WR
2025: Unanimous All-American · Cotton Bowl: 7 rec, 157 yds, TD vs. a defense built to stop him · Year 3 of being the first name on every game plan
The best player in college football, and the engine of everyone else's matchup. He forces the free safety to declare before every snap — which lightens the run box, opens the middle for the tight end, and hands every other receiver single coverage. The part casual fans miss: he's also one of the sport's most violent blocking receivers, which is why the perimeter run game works. The backstory matters too — he arrived as the most decorated receiver recruit of the internet era and has out-performed the hype, which almost never happens.

### How Opponents Will Attack Ohio State

The blueprint every staff has · and its expiration dates

#### 1Lie to Sayin

Show: 6-man pressure→Bring: 3, simulated
Make the pre-snap read correct and the post-snap read wrong. The half-count is the whole play — Miami proved it with a title berth on the line.

#### 2Bracket Smith Late

Pre-snap: single high→Post-snap: rotate the bracket
Make throwing to him expensive and throwing away from him uncomfortable. It worked twice in December, and both teams that ran it played for the title.

#### 3Attack the New LT

Games & twists→At a first-year blind side
The pressure point of a line that was nearly perfect for three months. Either a sophomore holds, or a guard plays out of position — pick your matchup.

#### 4Tempo the Rebuilt Defense

Sprint · motion · bunch→Hunt the nickel
Snap the ball before eight new starters finish talking. The Texas game exists in Week 2 for exactly this reason.
What Breaks the BlueprintHenry emerges → brackets get punished · Sayin's clock syncs → sim pressures die · the line communicates → twists stop landing · the defense calcifies (~week six) → tempo stops mattering. The season question: do the expiration dates arrive before October 17, or after?

#### Five Things That Are Actually True

No propaganda department

#### The season lives inside one arrow: 6→10.

Six sacks allowed in twelve games; ten in the last two. The teams that ended the season didn't out-talent Ohio State — they out-leveraged the line of scrimmage. Every offseason move answers that.

#### One lineman's position determines the ceiling.

If sophomore Ian Moore holds at left tackle, Austin Siereveld stays at right guard and the interior December attacked gets reinforced. If not, the domino runs backward. Where Siereveld lines up Week 1 tells you the ceiling by October.

#### The Texas trip is scheduled before the defense exists.

Eight new starters, game two, at night, against the most experienced quarterback in America. The timing favors the other sideline exactly once all year — which is why the loss is penciled here and the ranking barely moves. Rankings measure the team. Schedules measure the road.

#### October 17 through November 7 decides everything.

At Indiana, bye, at USC, Oregon at home — three top-14 opponents in 22 days, two time zones. Stress rating: 9.4 of 10, the highest three-week stretch we charted for any contender. Survive it 2-1 or better and the playoff math is comfortable.

#### The kicking operation is on notice, and nobody else will say it.

The season ended 13-10. In a three-point loss, every missed kick from the previous three months becomes a co-author. Not naming names, not piling on a kid — the group, as a group, has to be better.
