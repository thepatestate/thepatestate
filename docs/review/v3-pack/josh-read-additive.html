Miami Is the ACC Favorite, and the Alternatives Don't Hold Up

Miami enters 2026 at No. 7 in both major preseason polls off a 13-3 season, while the teams floated as alternatives — Clemson (7-6), SMU (9-4), Louisville (9-4) — carry thinner cases. The claim gets tested at Clemson on Oct. 3.

Spencer wrote in to say one of the bad predictions floating around is that Miami is by far the best team in the ACC and everyone else is playing for second. I agree with the prediction, not the criticism. Miami is the best team in the ACC by far, everyone else is playing for second, and the burden is on the other side of the argument: "So my question rhetorically, because you're not here to defend yourself, would be if not them, then who?"

Miami is No. 7 in the AP poll and No. 7 in the AFCA Coaches poll, the only ACC team ranked inside the top 15 of either, coming off 13-3 overall and 6-2 in the league. The counter-argument — that the Hurricanes were an overwhelming favorite last year and didn't finish it, that they still have not won an ACC Championship Game — is a fair historical point. But being nervous about a favorite isn't the same as naming a better one.

So take the three teams I floated as hypotheticals and look at them. Clemson, the program I said was in the mix last year, went 7-6 and 4-4 in the ACC in 2025. It is unranked in the AP poll and No. 23 in the Coaches poll. Clemson has the roster, the staff and the history to be a top-10 team by November — Sammy Brown and Peyton Streko and Bryant Wesco Jr. are not the problem — but a team coming off a .500 conference record and opening at LSU on Sept. 5 is not the safer pick. SMU has the best résumé of the alternatives: 9-4, 6-2 in the league, No. 19 AP and No. 20 Coaches, with Kevin Jennings back and a road trip to Florida State on Sept. 7 that will tell you plenty. Louisville sits at 9-4 overall but 4-4 in the ACC, and is No. 24 in the AP poll. Those are good teams. Two of them are ranked behind Miami by 12 and 17 spots. If your position is that one of them is the actual league favorite, you are asking me to move a 13-win team down and a 9-4 team up on the basis of last year's championship game.

The honest caveat is the one I gave on the show. Darian Mensah is the difference between Miami being the clear favorite and Miami being one of six teams. Behind him the depth chart thins out considerably, and the offense is built around a quarterback who can carry a game plan — Malachi Toney and Cooper Barkate on the outside, Mark Fletcher Jr. and CharMar Brown behind him, Jackson Cantwell and Samson Okunlola up front. Nobody has a reliable way to forecast availability at that position, and I'm not pretending otherwise. But a favorite going down to injury is a risk every favorite in America carries. It isn't an argument for anyone else.

There is one place where this site's own record cuts against my take, and I'd rather flag it than let a careful listener catch it. Our August preseason bracket has Clemson at No. 3 nationally and Miami at No. 11. That is a real tension. My read is that the bracket is a national projection with a heavy weight on ceiling and roster talent, while the ACC-favorite question is about who is most likely to be standing in Charlotte — and on that narrower question, the 13-win team with the better quarterback situation and the higher poll floor is where I am. But both of those can't be right after Oct. 3, and I'm not going to pretend they're the same claim.

Miami goes to Clemson on Saturday, Oct. 3 — the same building, the same program that spoiled things a year ago — and then plays Florida State on Oct. 17, Pittsburgh on Oct. 24, at North Carolina on Oct. 31, and at Notre Dame on Nov. 7 at 7:30 ET. Five weeks, four of them genuinely difficult, before a November close of Duke, Virginia Tech and Boston College that looks manageable on paper. If the Hurricanes come out of that Clemson trip and the Chapel Hill trip at full strength, the ACC race is functionally settled by Halloween and everything I said on the show holds. If they lose at Clemson, then Spencer has the argument he didn't quite make, and Clemson's No. 3 on our bracket looks a lot smarter than my No. 7 poll citation.

Not that Miami is guaranteed, but after you write down the records and the rankings, nobody has handed me a better name.

Why I Have Miami Beating Oregon for the Title — and What Has to Happen First

I moved Miami from the 11 seed on my own preseason bracket to the national championship in Las Vegas. Here are the dated games that will tell us whether that move was right, starting with Texas–Ohio State on Sept. 12 and Miami at Notre Dame on Nov. 7.

I picked the Miami Hurricanes to beat Oregon for the national championship, and I picked Mario Cristobal to do it against the program he left. That is the end of a bracket that runs USC into Tuscaloosa, Texas Tech into Austin, Oklahoma into Eugene, and then asks a group of coaches the internet has decided cannot win a title to go win one anyway.

This is a move off my own on-record projection, so here is the accounting. My preseason bracket in August seeded this field 1 Georgia, 2 Ohio State, 3 Clemson, 4 Boise State, 5 Texas, 6 Oregon, 7 Penn State, 8 LSU, 9 Notre Dame, 10 Alabama, 11 Miami, 12 Indiana, with Georgia 31, Ohio State 28 in the final. Miami was the eleventh-best team on that sheet. It is now my national champion. That is not a small adjustment and I am not going to pretend it is.

It is also less of a leap than the seeding suggests. Miami went 13-3 last year, 6-2 in the ACC, and opens the season No. 7 in both the AP Top 25 and the coaches poll. That is not a bubble team sneaking into the twelfth slot; that is a top-ten roster that got pushed to the back of a bracket by the math of at-large ordering. The elevation is real, but the floor under it was always higher than an 11 seed implies.

Where the bracket gets uncomfortable for me is the pecking order I picked against. Georgia opens at No. 3 in both polls off a 12-2, 7-1 season. Texas is No. 5 in the AP and No. 4 in the coaches poll off 10-3. Both of those teams are rated above Miami and above Oregon right now, and my bracket has Georgia going one-and-done out of a first-round bye and Texas losing in the semifinal. I still think Texas has it in them to rev the engine at the right time. I also think that is a projection about timing, which is the least reliable thing anyone projects in August.

Start with Texas–Ohio State. I called it a regular-season rematch in the bracket, and the regular-season half arrives Saturday, Sept. 12 at 7:30 p.m. ET in Austin. Week 2. That is the cleanest data point on the board for two of my four semifinalists. Texas has to prove the front can hold up in a game where the margin lives on the interior, and Ohio State has to prove a young quarterback can operate on the road at night in that building. Whoever wins does not clinch anything in September, but a Texas loss there means my Texas-over-Ohio-State projection in January is running against a result, not toward one.

Then Miami. The measuring stick is exactly the team I have advancing to the semifinal: Miami plays at Notre Dame on Saturday, Nov. 7 at 7:30 p.m. ET. Same matchup I put in my final four, played in South Bend in November instead of a neutral dome. There is a road trip to Clemson on Oct. 3 before it and Florida State at home on Oct. 17, so Miami will have been tested in the league. But Notre Dame in November is the checkpoint. If Miami's offensive line can hold up against a front that gets after the passer and the run game can stay ahead of schedule in cold-weather down-and-distance, the "on a mission" read I have on this team gets evidence. If Miami gets pushed around up front, my title pick is a vibe and I will say so.

And on the Kirby part of this: before anyone gets to argue about a third straight one-and-done out of a bye, Georgia has to survive at Alabama on Oct. 10 and at Ole Miss on Nov. 7, with Oklahoma at home on Sept. 26 in between. That is a genuine gauntlet, and it matters to my bracket in both directions. A Georgia team that comes out of that stretch at 11-1 arrives at the playoff as the No. 1 seed with a case that it is the best team in the country, which makes my Oregon-over-Georgia quarterfinal a real upset rather than a stylistic hunch. A Georgia team that drops one of those two road games probably is not sitting on a bye at all, and the entire top of my bracket reshuffles.

The pick stands: Miami over Oregon in Las Vegas. Three dates decide it — Sept. 12 in Austin, Oct. 10 in Tuscaloosa, Nov. 7 in South Bend — and by the second week of November we will know whether I moved a team eleven seeds for the right reasons.

The Big Ten and SEC Aimed Their NFL-Cut Policies at Schools, Not Players

Both leagues moved inside 24 hours, and both wrote the penalty toward institutions — coach suspensions, institutional fines. That splits the coming litigation into two tracks, and it changes the math for an LSU team already ranked No. 11 in the AP poll.

The Big Ten and the SEC did something in one Tuesday that they have not done on almost anything else in five years: they agreed, quickly, in writing, with their presidents lined up behind it. The Big Ten went past a statement and put out a policy — head-coach suspensions of up to half a season, institutional fines in the millions, unanimous support from every president and every athletic director. The SEC followed at close of business and went further, extending it to basketball and citing the commissioner's "full and unlimited authority," including the ability to suspend a school from competition. My read of both documents is the same: the threat is not pointed at players. It is pointed at the schools.

I said on the show I expect players to sue conferences, maybe as early as tomorrow, and I stand on that. But if the penalty structure lands on coaches and institutions rather than on eligibility, there is a second legal track running alongside the first one. A player suing for the right to play is arguing eligibility. A school or a coach facing a half-season suspension and a seven-figure fine for signing that same player is arguing something else entirely — the limits of conference authority over a member institution, and whether a league can penalize a school for complying with a court order. One asks a judge to let a kid on the field. The other asks a judge to stop a commissioner from swinging. The conferences may well win the first fight and still spend a year defending the second one.

Lane Kiffin's argument Tuesday morning was that these particular players are a specific class the courts already addressed, and that a conference declining to follow a court order creates "some issues there." There is a germ of validity in that. The five-for-five rule change caught players who had already declared, and you can construct a fairness case for them. It just does not bear the weight of greater scrutiny once you say the plain thing out loud: these are athletes who were in NFL preseason camps and played in NFL preseason games. The SEC's language closed the gray area Lane was trying to open — tryouts, declarations, all of it.

What I keep circling back to is the risk-reward. LSU is not a program clawing for relevance. They are No. 11 in the AP poll and No. 13 in the Coaches Poll after a 7-6 season that went 3-5 in the league. Our own preseason bracket on this site has LSU seeded No. 8 in the twelve-team field, with Georgia beating Ohio State 31-28 in the final. By our own projection, LSU is already in. That is the part that makes the loophole play harder to justify, not easier. When Texas Tech fought for Brendan Sorsby, that was a quarterback — a season-altering, playoff-altering piece. Adding a defensive lineman who got cut in late August, when your own path to the bracket is already drawn, is a much smaller gain measured against a coach missing half a season and an institution writing a check.

LSU plays at Auburn on Oct. 24 at noon ET, a road trip against a team that finished 5-7 and 1-7 in the SEC. If any of this policy ever bites — a suspended head coach on a sideline, a fined institution, an ineligibility ruling landing midweek — that is the kind of game where it shows up first, because that is the kind of game where the depth chart matters more than the marquee does. Watch what happens between now and then. If nobody in either league signs one of these players by mid-October, the policy worked without ever being tested. If somebody does, we will find out inside a month whether "full and unlimited authority" means what it says.

I do not count on competitors to police themselves, and I never have. That is what rules are for. Now they have to survive the part where somebody makes them enforce it.
