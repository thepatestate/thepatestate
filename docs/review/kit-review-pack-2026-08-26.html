Thomas Castellanos at Texas Tech: Why the Run-First Quarterback Must Change the Offense Before He Changes the Depth Chart

Castellanos arrives from Florida State with starting experience and a skillset that asks Texas Tech to do more than add another name to the room. The Red Raiders have to decide whether their offense can make room for what he does best without unsettling Will Hammond's current position.

The Pate State Staff · 790 words · Type · TP-05 · Thomas Castellanos' fit at Texas Tech · Architecture · magazine-feature

Thomas Castellanos arrives in Lubbock with a quarterback argument already attached to him. He is a former Florida State starter, he brings a run-first skillset, and Texas Tech has not announced whether he is joining the room as competition, insurance, or something in between.

That uncertainty isn't a flaw in the story. It is the story.

Will Hammond currently leads the Texas Tech quarterback room. Castellanos gives the Red Raiders another Power Four option behind him, but the value of that addition depends on whether Texas Tech is willing to build around Castellanos's strengths instead of asking him to operate as a duplicate of the quarterback already in place.

Josh Pate has described the pressure around Texas Tech this way: “A lot of folks could go 10 and two and have a ticker tape parade. That's not what would happen in Lubbock, Texas.”

That standard makes the quarterback decision heavier than a depth-chart update. Texas Tech needs answers that hold up when the offense has to stay on schedule, when a drive stalls, and when the opponent takes away the first thing the quarterback wants to do.

The offense has to make a choice

A run-first quarterback changes the questions an offense asks before the snap. Can the quarterback keep the ball on a read-option look? Can the offense create a numbers advantage by making the defense account for him in the run game? Can the passing structure give him throws that arrive quickly enough that the defense can't sit on the quarterback run?

Those choices are connected. If Texas Tech adds designed quarterback runs but leaves the rest of the offense untouched, the change can become a small package instead of a real answer. The defense can treat Castellanos as a specialty option, load the box when he enters, and force the Red Raiders to win with the part of the offense they were trying to avoid asking him to carry.

That doesn't mean the entire playbook has to be rebuilt around one player. It means the staff has to decide which parts of the existing offense survive when Castellanos is on the field and which parts need to move. Protection rules may change. Run-pass options may become more central. The quarterback's running threat may create room for a receiver, but only if the formation and timing make the defense honor both possibilities.

None of that has been announced as Texas Tech's plan. The point is narrower. Castellanos's value cannot be measured only by whether he can execute the current offense. Texas Tech has to measure what the current offense can become with him in it.

Hammond makes the decision more delicate

Hammond's standing matters because Castellanos isn't entering an empty room. Texas Tech already has a quarterback who leads the group, so the staff isn't choosing between a known starter and no answer. It is deciding how much of the offense belongs to the quarterback at the front of the room and how much flexibility the newcomer deserves.

There are two bad ways to handle that. Texas Tech can preserve the existing structure so completely that Castellanos becomes an emergency name on a depth chart. Or it can reshape too much before Castellanos has established that the new version works, creating uncertainty where the program previously had a plan.

The useful answer lives in the football, not the announcement. If Castellanos's presence gives Texas Tech a credible way to add a quarterback run without sacrificing its passing answers, he becomes more than insurance. If his role requires a separate offense that the staff only trusts in isolated situations, the room starts moving toward controversy because the roster will contain two different ideas about how the position should be played.

Texas Tech has not announced Castellanos's eligibility, his role, or whether Hammond will remain the starter. No public timetable or additional roster details have been provided. Those are not small omissions. They leave the most important football decision open: whether the staff sees Castellanos as a player to fit into the offense or a reason to alter it.

Texas Tech should adapt enough to use Castellanos's running threat, but not so much that it abandons the quarterback who currently leads the room. The Red Raiders need one offense with enough flexibility for both possibilities, not two separate systems competing for Saturday snaps.

The next public checkpoint is Texas Tech's official decision on the quarterback room. No date has been announced, so there is no result to grade yet in The Ledger. When that decision arrives, the evidence will be whether Castellanos receives a real part of the offense or only a backup label.

Florida Football Under Jon Sumrall: Five Questions About DJ Lagway, Defensive Identity, Transfers, Discipline, and September

Jon Sumrall inherits a Florida roster assembled for a different staff and a different set of answers. The Gators’ first season under him will turn on whether those answers can be rebuilt before the calendar starts charging interest.

The Pate State Staff · 1421 words · Type · PS-05 · Florida's biggest questions entering Jon Sumrall's first season · Architecture · magazine-feature

A new coach gets introduced with a handshake, a press conference, and several months of people telling him the roster is talented. Then September arrives and asks whether the roster fits what he wants to run.

Jon Sumrall’s first Florida season begins in that space. The Gators are changing leadership, and the roster was built around the previous staff’s plans. Nobody outside the building can responsibly name the depth-chart answers yet. The useful way to judge the transition is more specific: five questions, five fall checkpoints, and five places where the football will tell us whether Florida is settling or still searching.

1. Can DJ Lagway play the position Sumrall needs

Quarterback fit comes before quarterback talent. Florida needs to determine whether DJ Lagway’s best answers match the demands Sumrall places on the offense, especially when a defense changes the picture after the snap.

That means more than counting strong throws in warmups. It means seeing whether Lagway gets the ball out on time when the first read is covered, whether he protects the offense on early downs, and whether his feet keep the protection problem from becoming the whole afternoon. A quarterback can possess every attractive trait in the catalog and still make an offense work too hard if the decisions arrive late. > "DJ Lagway will ball out in 2026. Baylor wins the Big 12 and DJ Lagway wins the Heisman. Okay, well, that's a 10. That's a 10."

Pate has already put a loud prediction on Lagway’s 2026 season. Sumrall does not need Lagway to perform a prediction. He needs him to make the offense predictable for his own teammates and difficult for the defense.

The first checkpoint comes in Florida’s opening September game. By the end of September, the useful evidence will be third-down decisions, red-zone possessions, and how often the offense stays on schedule after the defense removes the first option. If those possessions are clean, the transition has a quarterback to build around. If they are chaotic, every other offensive decision gets harder.

2. What will Florida’s defense be known for

A new staff does not need a slogan. It needs eleven players making the same decision quickly.

Florida’s defensive identity will show up in ordinary snaps. Safeties will communicate a coverage adjustment before the snap. Linebackers will pass routes across the formation without leaving space behind them. The front will know who owns the quarterback when pressure changes late. Those details decide whether a defense plays fast or spends the afternoon correcting itself.

Sumrall inherited players who learned football through another staff’s language. The issue is not whether those players are capable. The issue is how much of their old training transfers when the calls, leverage points, and run fits change.

The first September games should reveal the direction. By mid-October, Florida should have a recognizable answer on third down. Does the defense force quarterbacks to hold the ball, or does it need pressure to cover for confusion? Does it tackle well enough to finish ordinary plays, or does one missed fit turn a short gain into a drive? Those are observable traits, not preseason branding.

The Gators can survive a defense that is still growing. They cannot survive one that makes the offense defend short fields every week.

3. Can the transfers become teammates before they become names

Transfer additions arrive with résumés. They become useful when the left guard knows how the center will handle a stunt, when a safety understands the corner’s help, and when a receiver adjusts his route without waiting for the sideline to explain the picture.

That learning happens under speed. A player can know the playbook and still be late to the right spot because he has not yet learned how a new teammate communicates under pressure. Florida’s roster transition makes that timing especially important. Sumrall is not simply adding players to an established structure. He is asking inherited players and newcomers to form one football vocabulary while the season is already counting.

The first September checkpoint should be simple: are new pieces helping Florida play faster, or are they creating extra hesitation around them? By the end of October, the evidence should be visible in substitution clarity, protection communication, and third-and-medium execution. If the same errors keep appearing with different players involved, the problem belongs to the structure around the unit, not one kid.

This is where talent gets converted into football. Until then, it is just a promising list of names.

4. Does discipline become part of the program’s daily life

Discipline is not a speech delivered after a penalty. It is whether players line up correctly when tired, finish a play without giving away fifteen yards, and understand the difference between aggression and freelancing.

For a first-year staff, that standard has to survive the moments when the game becomes uncomfortable. A false start after a long drive, a late hit after a stop, or a missed assignment following a substitution tells us more than a clean opening series. Those are the snaps where a team reveals whether it knows what it is doing or is relying on adrenaline.

Florida’s first meaningful read on this should come during the opening month, when opponents have enough film to test communication and enough confidence to make the Gators play through mistakes. A September penalty count alone will not settle the matter. The better measure is whether errors repeat in the same situations and whether the next possession shows a correction.

By the middle of October, discipline should look less like a coach’s demand and more like a player’s default. If Florida keeps losing downs to alignment errors, procedure penalties, and emotional decisions, the transition is not smooth no matter how attractive the roster looks.

Talent gives a team options. Discipline keeps those options available on third down.

5. How much time does the schedule give the transition

The schedule is not merely a list of opponents. It decides how long Florida can learn in public before a difficult game turns every unfinished answer into a national conversation.

The early tests matter because they stress different parts of the change. One game may ask whether Lagway handles pressure. Another may expose communication in the secondary. A road setting can turn protection checks into a problem before the ball is snapped. A physical opponent can tell us whether Florida’s front can hold its gaps when the offense is not generating easy possessions.

There is no supplied game-by-game calendar here to dress up with invented certainty, so the dates should remain honest. Kickoff arrives in early September. The first September stretch will show whether the new language travels from practice to Saturday. The end of September should reveal whether the staff is correcting recurring problems or merely surviving them. Mid-October is the point at which a team should begin looking like itself, even if the record has not answered every question.

Florida does not need to be finished by the first Saturday. It does need to be recognizable by October. If the same protection confusion, coverage busts, substitution mistakes, or discipline errors survive into that window, the schedule has stopped giving the transition room to grow.

A Florida season that can be read in real time

Sumrall’s first year will not be judged by one preseason impression. It will be judged by whether the Gators improve in the exact places that change first exposes: quarterback timing, defensive communication, newcomer coordination, daily discipline, and performance when the schedule applies pressure.

Pate has Florida in a broader preseason conversation because the roster contains talent. That makes the transition more consequential, not less. A talented roster can give a new staff enough answers to win early. It can also hide poor fit until the opponent finds the weakest communication point.

Florida’s direction should be clear by mid-October. If Lagway is on time, the defense is communicating, transfers are playing without hesitation, and penalties are shrinking in the situations that matter, Sumrall has made the change functional. If those problems remain connected across units, the Gators are still carrying the previous structure into the new era.

Florida’s first season under Jon Sumrall will turn on how quickly the roster becomes one football team, especially in protection, defensive communication, and situational discipline.
