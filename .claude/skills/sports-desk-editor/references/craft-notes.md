## The moves (what they do that our copy does not)

### 1. The lead carries the news, the tension and the clock in one built sentence
> "The Southeastern Conference passed a rule Tuesday banning players who have been drafted by NFL, NBA or WNBA teams or listed on rosters in those leagues, joining the Big Ten in taking a strong stand against an unexpected surge of pro players trying to return to college with the NCAA mired in a sprawling legal fight over eligibility." — AP (#1)

> "Well-traveled quarterback Tommy Castellanos plans to play this season for No. 12 Texas Tech, the Big 12 Conference favorite that is set for returner Will Hammond to be its starter after transfer Brendan Sorsby was declared ineligible because of gambling." — AP (#2)

One sentence: what happened, when, who it joins, what it is pushing against. The second sentence is the distinction ("The SEC's rule goes beyond the one adopted earlier in the day by the Big Ten"). Ours open with a fact and a number and no pressure: "Kentucky is listed at +12500 to win the SEC championship."

### 2. Anchor in time and place
Datelines. "Tuesday." "Four days before the start of football season." "With under two weeks until the season-opener." "Six days before the first full college football Saturday." Every pro story tells you when you are. Ours are timeless ("is scheduled to open") because the writer is never told the date.

### 3. Characterize — a reporter gets a judgment, in the reporter's own words
> "The news was the latest in a head-spinning week of court rulings, emergency rulemaking sessions and player signings, all of which point to a college sports landscape in disarray." — AP (#1)
> "The SEC rule puts LSU on notice." — AP (#1)
> "A program that has lost its way" (Wisconsin) — Forde (#13)
> "Kiffin left Oxford like a fugitive, standing on a tarmac waiting for a booster's private plane" — The Ringer (#19)

A characterization is one plain clause that says what the facts add up to. It is not "why it matters" as a section and it is not a hedge. Our copy either refuses to characterize (stenography) or characterizes in abstractions ("a quarterback ceiling most teams cannot manufacture").

### 4. Quotes are people talking
> "I feel like I took a freaking gummy," said Sean McVay … "I don't even understand it." — AP (#1)
> "I mean, we're in the Etch A Sketch era. The season starts, you draw a picture, and it ends and you got to shake it up and draw it again," Moorhead said. — AP (#9)
> "I literally spent all day Friday crying," said Ocean Rideout, a Portland State soccer player. — Dellenger (#15)

The best quote comes early (paragraph 3–5), after a one-line setup that says who is talking and what prompted it. Quotes run three to five sentences when the person is good. The verb is "said" (or "told CBS Sports," "wrote in a text message to The Associated Press"). Quotes are chosen because a human sounds like a human in them, not because they prove a bullet point. Our copy uses a quote as evidence for the sentence before it, one sentence long, and never lets the person be funny or wrong.

### 5. Numbers argue in pairs
> "His August/September record is 13–6; in games thereafter he is 30–6." — Forde (#13)
> "Over the past three years, Oregon is 1-5 vs. teams that played in the national championship game. The Ducks are 37-0 against everybody else." — Auerbach (#17)
> "Ohio State's athletic department took in $336.1 million … Akron's revenues were less than one-tenth of that at $32.7 million." — AP (#9)

A number alone is a fact; two numbers next to each other are an argument. Our copy lists numbers ("3,100 yards, 500 on the ground, 31 touchdowns") without a contrast, so they read as filler.

### 6. Memory
> "Let's not forget that two of the best Tennessee teams in recent memory lost in those venues. The 2022 Volunteers were upset by the Gamecocks, and the 2024 College Football Playoff team fell to the Razorbacks." — CBS (#11)
> "Cal has topped 60,000 fans just four times since the remodeled stadium reopened in 2012." — AP (#8)
> "Notre Dame is still the last team to beat the Hoosiers, dating to December 2024." — Forde (#13)

The reporter's value is remembering something the source did not say. The fact sheet is there for this; the writer should reach into it for a comparison, not a summary.

### 7. Texture: one detail nobody could have invented
The bounce house and the Korean fried chicken bao buns (#4). A fan serving as offensive coordinator for the first series, 23 million views (#9). Howard's Rock as a doorstop (#10). Hemingway's cats (#18). "Kiffin and Brown jokingly call each other out if one of them is at the facility without the other" (#5). One per story, placed where it lands, never explained.

### 8. Rhythm is uneven on purpose
> "Exclusion led to reflection. And motivation. The more days Notre Dame has had to simmer in its discontent, the more useful it might be in 2026." — Forde (#13)
> "That's right." (a two-word paragraph) — Dellenger (#16)
> "Thing is, Akron isn't going to change now." — AP (#9)

Paragraphs run from one sentence to six. Sentences run from two words to fifty. Fragments are allowed. Our copy's paragraphs are all 2–3 sentences of 18–24 words, which is the single most reliable machine tell.

### 9. What happens next is a sentence with a date, not a section
> "The other Power Four leagues, the Atlantic Coast and Big 12 conferences, are expected to discuss the issue Wednesday." — AP (#1)
> "But that bill is weeks away from a vote. Football season starts Saturday, with the featured game taking place in Ireland." — AP (#1)
> "Meanwhile, the NFL must cut their final rosters to 53 players by Aug. 30 — six days before the first full college football Saturday." — Dellenger (#16)

### 10. The kicker
End on the best remaining quote, a wry line, or the thesis said harder:
> "Well, everything but the parking." — Dodd (#18)
> "It's a mess," said Darren Heitner … / "I need to figure this s*** out. It's such a wild time." — Dellenger (#16)
> "Changing that mentality is impossible in a loose confederation of fiefdoms with no central oversight." — Forde (#14)
> "If Notre Dame is going to put its long, painful offseason to productive use, a fast start is nonnegotiable." — Forde (#13)

Never end on the schedule. Never end on "will show whether." Never summarize.

### 11. Verbs
Pros: passed, banning, tamp down, puts on notice, popped into the portal, snagged, churned out, punished defenders, scramble, careening, flouting. Ours: is listed, is projected, remains, enters, is among, gives, provides. A stative verb is fine once; a story built on them is a table.

### 12. Sourcing texture instead of self-audit
> "sources confirmed to CBS Sports' Matt Zenitz" / "Sources tell CBS Sports the punishment is modeled on the NCAA's ghost transfer rule." (#12)
> "The SEC did not immediately divulge its penalties, though commissioner Greg Sankey is expected to have wide latitude." — AP (#1)
> "Though he was careful not to confirm any new signings, Kiffin addressed the situation" — Dellenger (#15)

When something is unknown, a reporter says who didn't say it. Our copy writes "The reporting does not establish Kiffin's exact compensation," which is an editor's note that leaked into the story.

### 13. Columns: the thesis is the writer's, early, and it has a nickname
> "What's best for the sport as a whole is disconnected from what's best for roughly 68 individual fiefdoms, and the millionaire athletic directors and coaches leading them. They want easy, even if easy undermines the best parts of college football." — Forde (#14)
> "They'd prefer the Big Ten Easy Button Playoff, with 24 teams." / "the friendly local judge strategy" / "Will Wade, longstanding exemplar of NCAA compliance virtue" — Forde (#14)

The columnist is in the room (dateline, "here"), names the thing with attitude, argues through reported quotes and paired numbers, allows one aside in parentheses "(gah) 24", and ends on the thesis with more force than it started. No mandatory concession paragraph; no "what I'm watching" section; no "the test is dated."

### 14. Explanatory pieces talk to the reader
> "You're probably wondering how in the hell this is happening." / "If you aren't connecting the dots…" / "But there is more to this entire situation — much, much more." — Dellenger (#16)
Direct address, detective structure, one reveal at a time.

### 15. Human stakes lead when there are humans
Dellenger (#15) opens on one athlete's morning — told at breakfast he is ineligible, a $50,000 NIL deal gone — before a word about courts. The Akron feature (#9) opens on a former president's quote from 1987. The person, then the policy.

## The forms and their shapes

- **Item (75–200 words).** Petitti (#6): news sentence with the who/when; one quote from the person who made the decision; two paragraphs of what he has done and what the extension means (the next media deal, 2030). No schedule paragraph unless the schedule is the news.
- **Brief / story (200–750).** Castellanos (#2), Cal tarps (#8), Golesh (#5): lead with tension → the distinction → the best quote → the history/pair of numbers → what's next with a date → a quote or a wry kicker.
- **Reported feature (600–1,300).** Reed (#4), BYU (#3), Akron (#9): a scene or a person first; quotes carry 40–60% of the words; the reporter's memory and one uninventable detail; ends on the person.
- **Column (800–2,400).** Forde (#13, #14): dateline; thesis in the writer's voice by paragraph 3; the reporting (quotes, paired numbers, memory) as the argument; nicknames and asides; the kicker is the thesis, harder.
